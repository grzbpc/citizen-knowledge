### The story

Metro reports that new Cabinet Office guidance, marked "sensitive" and withheld from publication, would require trans civil servants to use toilets and facilities matching their biological sex rather than their affirmed gender, even for those holding a Gender Recognition Certificate. Staff who deliberately misgender colleagues could face action for misconduct. The guidance states there is "no legal requirement for you to tell anybody" that a person is trans, but staff must discuss "the most suitable arrangement" with their line manager, an arrangement the document says "cannot be decided by you alone." Steph Richards of advocacy group TransLucent warned of a "coercive trap," where trans staff may be forced to "out" themselves to managers to comply with the new rules. The Good Law Project's Jess O'Thomson said trans people will have to explain to colleagues why they are suddenly using different facilities, even after years of using the ones matching their affirmed gender.

### The reframe, with the manuscript

The government's framing does a lot of work here. There is "no legal requirement" to tell anybody, the guidance says, as though that settles the matter. But a rule that is not compulsory in law can still be compulsory in practice if the alternative is an arrangement the member of staff is not permitted to settle alone, and if the trigger for enforcement is a colleague's decision to misgender them and not their own decision to disclose.

The manuscript was not written about this guidance, but its account of rules that cannot be applied cleanly is a useful comparison. Writing about policing, it says where the difficulty of an incoherent rule ends up.

> *When laws are too broad, contradictory, or disconnected from everyday behaviour, enforcement cannot be uniform. Officers are left to decide which rules matter, when they matter, and to whom they apply.*

That passage is about officers, and the people here are civil servants and line managers, so the comparison has limits. The shape is the same. The guidance does not settle who may use which facilities in a way a person could simply follow. It says the arrangement "cannot be decided by you alone" and leaves it to be worked out between a member of staff and a manager. The contradiction between a rule that denies any duty to disclose and a process that makes disclosure the practical route is not removed. It is passed to the individuals nearest to it, which in the manuscript's phrase is how the burden of coherence moves downward.

The second comparison is about uncertainty. The manuscript's discussion of rules whose limits are never stated says who carries it.

> *Responsibility for uncertainty is displaced onto those subject to the rule.*

Metro reports that the guidance is marked "sensitive" and has not been published, so the exact wording of the misconduct provision cannot be checked here. On what has been reported, the clause on deliberate misgendering sounds like protection, and it may well be one. It is also the enforcement half of a policy whose disclosure half is voluntary in name. Nobody is required to tell their employer they are trans. A decision about facilities still has to be made, and the reporting suggests it carries a formal consequence if a colleague does not respect it.

Richards's phrase, "coercive trap", is a description from the advocacy side, and the Cabinet Office position, as reported, is that there is no requirement to tell anybody. This entry does not settle that dispute. It notes where the weight falls if the reporting is accurate. A trap does not need a locked door, only that every visible exit costs something the person was not required to pay a week earlier.
