### The story

Gatwick airport lost all running water on Sunday, on one of the busiest travel days of the year and days after many schools broke up. Toilets could not be flushed and were locked once they became blocked. Drinking fountains stopped working. Restaurants, lounges and bars were forced to shut at both terminals of the UK's second busiest airport, because no food or drink could be prepared, and crowds were left sitting on the floor. Airport management said bottled water was being handed out and that staff were on hand to support passengers, adding that some restaurant partners had opened to provide additional seating. Passengers described something else. Charlotte Anstey, heading to Spain, said there was no announcement and that she learned what had happened from a restaurant employee, adding that guards were blocking access to the toilets and that there was no sign of the promised water bottles. Jen Brook, stuck in the airport from 9.30am to 5.30pm after a cancellation, said pregnant and elderly passengers were being turned away from locked toilets, and reported one member of staff telling her to go and complain about it. The outage was caused by a power failure at Sutton and East Surrey Water's Bough Beech treatment works. The company, which has more than 740,000 customers, reported at 1.45pm that the fault was fixed but said the water still needed treating before services could return to normal. Its owner, Pennon, was fined in 2023 for illegally dumping sewage into rivers and seas in Devon and Cornwall on 37,649 occasions.

### The reframe, with the manuscript

Consider what the airport was and was not able to do on Sunday. It could not produce water. That is genuinely outside its control, the consequence of a power failure at a treatment works belonging to a company it does not own. What it could do, and did, within hours, was staff the doors of the locked toilets, close the outlets, and issue a statement describing assistance that several passengers say never materialised. The capacity to resolve the failure was absent. The capacity to administer it was intact and immediately available.

Chapter 9 describes this asymmetry as the ordinary condition of institutional life rather than a lapse:

> *This pattern illustrates a broader institutional tendency: resolution is difficult, but administration is always possible. Not because solutions are unknown, but because meaningful resolution would require confronting incentives far upstream.*
> <span class="cite">The Performance of Obedience, Ch. 9</span>

The incentives upstream of Sunday are not obscure. An airport handling tens of thousands of passengers a day depends on a single water supplier, whose treatment works depends on a power supply, with no arrangement in place capable of keeping lavatories functioning for the several hours between failure and restoration. Nobody at Gatwick decided that this should be so. It was inherited, contracted, and left alone, because the cost of building genuine redundancy is immediate and the cost of a failure is borne almost entirely by passengers who have already paid and cannot leave. Sunday was the day the arrangement was tested, and the result was a queue of families sitting on the floor of a terminal in front of shuttered restaurants, being told that support was on hand.

The detail that ought to stay with the reader is the guards. Deploying staff to prevent people from entering blocked lavatories is not an absence of institutional capacity. It is an exercise of it, and a fairly efficient one. Somebody assessed the risk, decided that unusable toilets presented a greater liability open than closed, and allocated people to enforce that decision. The same organisation could not tell passengers what was happening before they passed through security. Enforcement was available. Information was not. That ordering of priorities is the whole story, and it is not unusual enough to be scandalous.

Set alongside this the position of the supplier's owner. Pennon was fined in 2023 for illegally dumping sewage on 37,649 separate occasions. The company continues to operate, continues to hold the licence, and continues to supply the airport. The passengers who could not wash their hands on Sunday, by contrast, would have been removed from the premises had they responded to the situation in the obvious way, and the cleaners were reportedly stopping them from doing so. Chapter 2 names the principle at work:

> *When enforcement is inconsistent, rules stop signalling responsibility and start signalling vulnerability. Punishment no longer teaches norms; it teaches positioning. People are not sanctioned for violating standards, but for violating them without insulation.*
> <span class="cite">The Performance of Obedience, Ch. 2</span>

A fine of any size imposed once on a water company is a cost. It appears in a set of accounts, is absorbed, and does not interrupt operations. The equivalent consequence for an individual is removal, prosecution, or a criminal record, and it interrupts everything. Neither outcome is secret. Both are lawful. They are simply two different enforcement regimes running in the same country on the same day, and the position a person occupies determines which of them they meet.

This is the second supply failure in this region to appear in this record in a week. On 20 July, about 7,000 properties across Kent and Sussex lost water after a shutdown at South East Water's Tunbridge Wells works, days after that company was ordered to pay £30.5million for previous interruptions in the same area. That was a different operator, which is the point rather than a qualification. Two separate companies, two separate networks, one week apart, produced the same result for the people at the end of the pipe, and in both cases the accountability mechanism produced an apology and a statement about working to resolve the issue. Chapter 9 is precise about what a society is saying when it can do this repeatedly without changing anything:

> *A society that can manage visible failure indefinitely without resolving it is signalling something precise: that the appearance of order matters more than the reduction of harm; that stability is preferred to justice; that containment is safer than change.*
> <span class="cite">The Performance of Obedience, Ch. 9</span>

None of this requires anybody at Gatwick, at Sutton and East Surrey Water, or at Pennon to have behaved badly on Sunday. The staff member who told a passenger to go and complain was rude, and rudeness under those conditions is a symptom rather than a cause. The people on the terminal floor were dealing with employees who had no more information than they did and no authority to fix anything. What the day exposed was the shape of the arrangement rather than the character of anyone inside it: a chain of contracts in which every party can point to a party further up, an obligation to supply water that carries no operational consequence when the water stops, and a regulator whose most serious available response has already been used, at scale, without altering the behaviour it was imposed for.

By the end of the day the water was returning and the airport was functioning. Nothing that produced Sunday has changed, and there is no mechanism currently in view that would change it before the next power failure at the next treatment works.

> *Failure is not solved. It is administered.*
> <span class="cite">The Performance of Obedience, Ch. 9</span>
