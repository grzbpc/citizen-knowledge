### The story

A funeral director left 31 bodies, including a stillborn baby's, to decompose on an "almost industrial scale" at a parlour that became a horror scene, a court heard. Robert Bush, 48, took money for cremations but did not carry them out, then handed grieving families the wrong ashes to cover his tracks as he siphoned off the cash, and kept charity donations intended for other purposes. Karen King's father Norman Bridger, 94, was found ten months after she was handed ashes she was told were his. "It's beyond belief," she said. "How could someone be so cruel?" Bush's deception only ended in March 2024, when he was on holiday and needed another firm to collect a body from a care home. He told a casual worker not to let the undertakers in. When they grew suspicious and were eventually let inside Legacy Independent Funeral Directors in Hull, prosecutor Chris Paxton said a stretcher was found on the floor with evidence of human putrefaction in a mop and bucket nearby, other remains were in sealed wooden coffins, and one hundred sets of ashes were found. Bush blamed £90,000 of debts on a "cash-flow problem," saying people did not always pay, but turnover reported to HMRC did not reflect that. From March 2017 to April 2024, more than £3million was paid into his business and personal accounts, while profligate personal spending and transfers to his wife totalled £879,000. Bush has pleaded guilty to 67 charges including preventing a lawful and decent burial and fraud. A five-day sentencing hearing began yesterday. Many of the families are campaigning for the funeral industry to be regulated.

### The reframe

Notice the detail buried at the end of the report: families are campaigning for the funeral industry to be regulated, which means, stated plainly, that it currently is not. Anyone can open a funeral parlour, take payment for cremations, issue invoices, and hand over ashes, without a licensing body checking whether any cremation has actually taken place. The entire transaction, from the family's side, is conducted on trust in the appearance of professionalism: the premises, the uniform, the paperwork, the solemnity of the exchange. Nothing in that appearance requires verification, because nothing in the law requires the appearance to correspond to anything real.

This case did not fail because Bush was unusually skilled at deception. It failed because the mechanism that would normally catch this kind of fraud, someone with the standing to inspect what actually happens behind a funeral director's door, does not exist as a matter of routine. Thirty-one bodies accumulated over what the prosecution described as years. Ashes were issued that were not the right ashes, to families who had no way of knowing and no external body they could have checked with even had they suspected something was wrong. The £3million that moved through Bush's accounts between 2017 and 2024 did so without the turnover reported to HMRC matching the money actually taken in, and that mismatch, too, sat unexamined for years, because tax reporting anomalies in a single small business rarely trigger proactive scrutiny.

What finally exposed the parlour was not oversight. It was a scheduling accident: Bush went on holiday, a casual worker was left with an instruction not to admit anyone, and that instruction was unusual enough to make a different firm suspicious. Take that single absence out of the account and the operation had no natural expiry date. The families were doing everything asked of them, paying promptly, trusting the ceremony, believing the paperwork, and the system asked nothing further of the person receiving their money. That is the shape of a regulatory gap rather than an individual failure of vigilance: the checking was never anybody's job, so its absence produced no alarm, no report, and no institutional memory, right up until a member of staff happened to notice something odd on an ordinary Tuesday.
