### The story

Prime minister Andy Burnham has unveiled plans for a new bursary worth up to £4,500 a year for families on benefits, paid when their teenager takes up an apprenticeship, in a bid to reduce the number of young people not in education, employment or training. It follows a report warning the number of Neets aged 16 to 24 could hit one million by 2031. Burnham told reporters: "I cannot accept the situation where we have so many young people in their 20s on benefits. It's just not right for them. And why do we have that? The support just isn't there." From Year Ten, pupils will also be offered new technical education routes combining academic subjects with vocational training. Shadow education secretary Laura Trott said the plans are "all over the place."

### The reframe, with the manuscript

Look at the shape of the announcement rather than its size. The bursary is described, in the Metro's own headline, as a way to stop households "losing out on cash." That framing matters. It presents the payment as compensation for a loss that would otherwise occur, rather than as a reward for a specific choice a family has made. But the loss only occurs if the household does not take the apprenticeship route. A family whose teenager stays in sixth form, or takes an unpaid work placement, or simply struggles to find any apprenticeship place in their area, loses out on exactly the same £4,500, for a decision, or a circumstance, that was never described to them as a penalty.

The book's account of how conditional support actually operates is worth setting against Burnham's language here:

> *People are sanctioned not because their circumstances have improved or worsened, but because a form was late, an appointment was missed, or a requirement was not performed exactly as specified.*

A bursary is the reverse mechanism to a sanction, but it runs on the same logic. Nothing about a family's underlying need changes between the household whose child gets an apprenticeship place and the household whose child does not. What changes is compliance with a specific, narrow pathway the government has decided to reward. The £4,500 does not track hardship. It tracks whether the teenager did the one thing the scheme was built around.

That distinction is easy to miss because the announcement is dressed in the language of protection rather than incentive. "So they don't lose out on cash" sounds like a floor being put under families who might otherwise fall through a gap. It is, in practice, a new and narrower gap, one that opens specifically for the families whose teenager's circumstances, ability, local labour market, or simple preference don't point toward an apprenticeship. The chapter's broader point applies directly:

> *In this framework, welfare is less a route to resolution than a structure for managing problems that remain unresolved.*

Reducing Neet numbers by one million is the stated aim. Whether this bursary reduces the underlying number of young people without a path, or simply reshuffles which families receive support based on which route their teenager happens to take, is a different question, and one the announcement does not really try to answer. Laura Trott's complaint that the plans are "all over the place" is itself a symbolic-politics response, criticism of presentation rather than of the underlying mechanism, but it inadvertently gestures at the same gap this book keeps returning to: a policy can be coherent as an announcement and incoherent as a lived experience, depending entirely on which side of the eligibility line a family happens to sit.
