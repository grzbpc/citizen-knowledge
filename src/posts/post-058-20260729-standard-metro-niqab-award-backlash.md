### The story

Khadija Patel, 39, who founded youth sports club Krimmz in Bolton and has spent years running sessions for boys and girls from ethnically diverse and under-represented backgrounds, has been abused online after being pictured wearing a niqab following a British Citizen Award ceremony at the Palace of Westminster, where she was one of 27 winners celebrating individuals who made contributions to their communities. Commentators including far-right activist Tommy Robinson criticised her for wearing face covering while working with children, with one claiming Ms Patel's dress "does not empower children" and told her to stop "serving me from now on." Ms Patel said: "We are a community. We may not make the same choices in terms of clothing, but we should listen to people and celebrate their work." She added: "It does not define the whole of who I am. I am still the same person as everyone in my community. I love promoting sport - the niqab does not limit me."

### The reframe

Ms Patel has been running Krimmz, working with children in Bolton, for long enough to build a club that runs sessions for boys and girls and was invited to receive a national award for it. None of that activity was contentious while it happened quietly. The backlash arrived at the exact moment her work became visible on a national stage, at the Palace of Westminster, photographed alongside a government minister.

The book's account of what triggers scrutiny under threshold logic is relevant here, even where the underlying rule is social rather than legal:

<blockquote class="quote--manuscript">Threshold logic follows. Behaviour below the level that attracts attention is tolerated. Once it crosses an informal boundary, the full force of the rule can be applied.<span class="cite">Chapter 2: Selective Enforcement and Threshold Logic</span></blockquote>

Nothing about Ms Patel's clothing changed between her years of unremarked volunteering and the week of the award. What changed was exposure. The niqab did not become newly incompatible with working with children; it became newly visible to an audience primed to object to it. That is not a claim about whether critics are right or wrong on the substance. It is a observation about timing, and timing is usually the more honest signal. If the objection were genuinely about children's welfare, it would have applied with equal force during every session Krimmz has run since the club was founded. It did not surface until the recognition did.

Ms Patel's own response, that people can disagree about clothing while still listening to and celebrating someone's work, describes a form of tolerance that operates until it becomes inconvenient to maintain in public. That gap, between private tolerance and public performance of concern, is where this pattern consistently lives.
