### The story

Joshua Bonehill-Paine, previously convicted of racially harassing the Jewish former MP Luciana Berger and jailed on other occasions for anti-Semitic hate offences, has been brought in by the Conservative Party to advise on extremism ahead of an election. The party has defended the appointment as reflecting his renunciation of his past views and his current usefulness in identifying and countering extremist activity. The move has been criticised by those who point to the seriousness and target of his original offending.

### The reframe

Whatever the merits of rehabilitation as a principle, and the manuscript does not argue against it, the question this appointment raises is not whether people can change. It is why this particular route back into institutional relevance was available to him at all, when it is not available in comparable form to most people convicted of comparable offences.

<blockquote class="quote--manuscript">
Those who grasp this early, through upbringing, education or proximity to power, move through institutions with relative ease. They learn which rules are symbolic, which are negotiable, and which apply only to those without protection.
<span class="cite">The Performance of Obedience, Ch. 2</span>
</blockquote>

A conviction for racially harassing a sitting MP is not a minor or ambiguous offence. It sits in the same category of behaviour that, for most people who commit it, closes doors permanently: employment, standing, institutional trust. What this appointment demonstrates is that the door was never closed absolutely. It was closed for as long as it was politically costly to open it, and reopened once someone judged the specific, individual case to carry more value as a resource than risk as a liability.

That judgement may turn out to be right in the narrow, operational sense: people convicted of extremist offences plainly can hold useful knowledge about how extremism operates. But the manuscript's point is not about whether the judgement was correct. It is about who gets the judgement made on their behalf at all. Most people who have crossed the same line do not get offered an advisory role. They get a record that follows them. The difference between the two outcomes was never really about the offence. It was about who was positioned, afterwards, to make the case for their own exception.
