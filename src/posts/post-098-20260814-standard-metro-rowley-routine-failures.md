### The story

Metropolitan Police commissioner Sir Mark Rowley has said serious offenders being freed back into the community near their victims is now happening "routinely," days after the case of double-murderer Simon Levy exposed a string of bail and recall failings (see yesterday's entry). Sir Mark told BBC's Today programme that the courts, Crown Prosecution Service and probation service are all struggling under the same pressures, with no single agency prepared to be publicly accountable for the outcome. He cited a recent case in which a sex offender was bailed to an address two doors from his victim. He said the Met is now roughly 4,000 officers smaller than four years ago even as there are 10,800 registered sex offenders in London, and described the situation as "a systemic disinvestment" across the agencies meant to keep people safe.

### The reframe

Yesterday's entry on Simon Levy treated his case as a sequence of individually defensible decisions that combined into something no single person would have signed off on if asked directly. Sir Mark Rowley's comments this week confirm that Levy was not an outlier. He used the word "routinely." That is not a complaint about a bad week. It is a description of the system's normal output.

Notice what does not follow from that admission. Naming a pattern as routine is not the same as proposing to change it. Nothing in Sir Mark's account suggests the answer on offer is a redesign of how bail, recall and remand decisions interact across four separate agencies. The answer on offer, so far, is more of the same machinery under more strain: more officers if funding allows, more prison capacity if the Ministry of Justice can find it, more coordination meetings between services that already know they are failing to coordinate. The machinery does not pause to ask whether it is working. It continues, understaffed and overstretched, because continuing is the only mode any of the four agencies knows how to operate in.

What makes Sir Mark's intervention notable is not that police failures happen. It is that the person running the institution responsible is the one naming the pattern publicly, rather than a journalist, a bereaved family or an inquiry years later. That itself says something about where accountability currently sits: it is easier for a serving commissioner to admit the system is routinely failing than for any one of the four agencies involved to accept the specific decision that made a specific case go wrong. Diffuse blame protects everyone in it. Naming the pattern without naming the decision protects the pattern.
