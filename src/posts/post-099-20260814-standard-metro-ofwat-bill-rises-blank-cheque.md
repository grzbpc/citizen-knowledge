### The story

Regulator Ofwat has said Thames Water, Severn Trent, Southern Water, Wessex Water and South East Water will be allowed to raise bills for millions of customers, with the increases intended to fund £3.4billion of improvements by 2030. Thames Water is heavily indebted. Prime minister Andy Burnham criticised the proposal, saying customers "cannot be treated as a blank cheque" and adding "I understand why people are angry, I am too." Ofwat will make its final ruling on the prospective bill rises in December.

### The reframe

Notice the sequence. The regulator sets out the increases. The prime minister objects, publicly and in the language of anger, before the decision he is objecting to has actually been made. Ofwat's ruling is not due until December. Mr Burnham's statement arrives now, months ahead of the outcome it appears to be resisting, at the exact moment it will attract the most attention and cost the least. Nothing in his intervention slows, blocks or amends the process it responds to.

The manuscript's account of symbolic politics describes precisely this timing: <blockquote class="quote--manuscript"><p>Housing targets are published long before homes exist. Crime strategies are launched before the institutions responsible for implementing them possess the resources required to do so. The political rewards arrive immediately. The practical consequences arrive later, if they arrive at all.</p><span class="cite">The Performance of Obedience, Ch. 14</span></blockquote>

Substitute "bill rise" for "housing target" and the mechanism is identical. The objection is announced immediately. The financial reality it concerns will not be settled for months, by an independent regulator whose process was already underway before the objection was voiced. By December, when Ofwat rules, the prime minister's August comments will have done their political work regardless of the outcome. If bills rise as proposed, he will have been shown to have "understood" the anger in advance. If they rise by less, he will be able to claim the objection had an effect. Either way, the statement costs him nothing now and can be cited as evidence of concern later.

None of this means the anger is manufactured, or that Thames Water's debt position and the case for investment are not real and serious questions. It means the prime minister's response and the regulator's decision are not, on this timeline, the same process. One is political theatre performed in real time. The other is a technical ruling that will land in December whatever is said in August. Trust rarely disappears because a single promise is broken. It erodes because a pattern becomes visible, and the pattern here is one this project has traced before: language arriving on a different clock to the decision it appears to address.
