### The story

Police seizures of e-bikes have soared by 83 per cent in a year to 7,049 across the UK, up from 3,858, as forces crack down on dangerous, illegally modified models. Many are souped up to exceed the legal limit of 15.5mph and have been involved in fatal crashes and street crime, including phone theft and mugging. Carlisle MP Julie Minns called them "the perfect accessory to phone theft, mugging and drug dealing" and is asking the prime minister to have them declared an "unsafe product" so they cannot be sold by online retailers. The Met seized the most e-bikes of any force, and Wiltshire Police said half its seizures were in Swindon, part of transport secretary Heidi Alexander's constituency.

### The reframe

The e-bike itself is not the problem. A legal, speed-limited model is sold openly on the high street, ridden by commuters, and treated as ordinary transport infrastructure. A modified version of the same machine, capable of the same journeys at higher speed, is being seized at a rate that has more than doubled in a year and linked in reporting to fatal crashes and street crime.

The manuscript's account of how modern institutions sort harm describes this kind of split precisely: <blockquote class="quote--manuscript"><p>One category of harm generates revenue. The other generates cost. The distinction between them is not scientific. It is administrative.</p><span class="cite">The Performance of Obedience, Ch. 11</span></blockquote>

The legal e-bike generates VAT, retail turnover, and a place in the government's own push toward active travel. The illegal one generates a seizure operation, a court appearance in some cases, and now a parliamentary request that it be banned from sale altogether. Both machines move a person through a city faster than walking. What separates them is not how dangerous either one is in isolation, since a legal e-bike ridden recklessly can kill just as easily as an illegal one ridden carefully. What separates them is whether the specific unit in question has been brought inside the regulatory and fiscal structure that governs speed limits, motor power, and retail sale.

Julie Minns's proposed fix, an "unsafe product" designation that would stop online retailers selling the illegal versions, is a request to move the administrative line, not to abolish it. That is a reasonable ask on its own terms. But it is worth noticing that the mechanism she is invoking, redefine the category and the enforcement regime changes overnight, is the same mechanism that currently protects the legal e-bike from any equivalent scrutiny. Nobody is proposing an "unsafe product" review of the machines currently earning retailers their margin. The category, once drawn, tends to stay drawn in whichever direction currently generates revenue rather than cost.
