### The story

US Army chiefs have been told to scale back annual military exercises with South Korea after Seoul did not support America's war with Iran. Donald Trump gave the order, according to reports, also citing his good relationship with North Korean dictator Kim Jong-un as a factor in the decision. Trump said Jong-un has been "unthreatening and respectful" during White House terms and that he finds the joint US-South Korea exercises "hostile." The change comes as Trump's separately declared 60-day deadline for peace talks with Iran expired with no resolution, having threatened to "bomb the s***" out of Oman if the country's brokered access route for Iranian oil tankers through the Strait of Hormuz interfered with US plans.

### The reframe, with the manuscript

Start with what actually happened here. South Korea, a formal treaty ally that has hosted joint exercises with the United States for over seven decades, declined to support a war it had no part in starting. In response, its annual military cooperation with its principal security guarantor was scaled back. Meanwhile Kim Jong-un, who leads a state under international sanctions for its nuclear weapons programme and has no formal alliance with Washington at all, is praised as "unthreatening and respectful," his objection to the same category of exercise treated as a reasonable position rather than a hostile one.

The manuscript's account of selective enforcement is built around exactly this kind of asymmetry, where the label applied to a behaviour depends less on the behaviour than on who is doing it:

> *What matters is not only the behaviour, but the circumstances around it: who is visible, who is expendable, who is insulated.*

South Korea is visible and, in this instance, expendable. It is a democracy that answers to its own public and its own alliance obligations, which means declining to join a war it was not asked to fight is a position it can actually state out loud, in public, through official channels. That transparency is precisely what makes it easy to penalise. Kim Jong-un answers to no comparable check. His approval or disapproval of joint exercises is not a diplomatic position weighed against competing obligations. It is simply accommodated, and accommodated in language, "unthreatening and respectful," that a democratic ally's considered decision not to join an unrelated war did not receive.

The manuscript's broader point about what selective enforcement actually produces is worth sitting with here:

> *A system that enforces rules unevenly does not reduce wrongdoing; it redistributes it.*

Nothing about this decision reduces any actual security risk on the Korean peninsula. What it does is redistribute the cost of a separate war, the one in Iran, onto an ally that had nothing to do with starting it, while redistributing goodwill toward a regime whose weapons programme is the entire reason the exercises exist in the first place. The word "hostile" is doing no descriptive work at all. It is simply attached to whichever party is currently less useful to accommodate.

This is the same asymmetry the manuscript describes when it turns from institutions to the individuals caught inside them, and the logic scales cleanly from a person navigating a rule to a state navigating an alliance:

> *The difference between those two people is not moral. It is structural.*

Substitute state for person and the sentence holds. South Korea did not do anything Kim Jong-un has not also done, declined to participate in an arrangement it judged not to serve its interests. What separates the two is not the substance of the position. It is which one had the standing to be punished for holding it, and which one Washington currently needs more than it needs the appearance of consistency.
