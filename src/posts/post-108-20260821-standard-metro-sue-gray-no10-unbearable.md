### The story

Baroness Sue Gray, who was Sir Keir Starmer's chief of staff before quitting in 2024, has said working in No.10 was "unbearable" as she explained the pressures leading to her resignation. Speaking on the Political Currency podcast, Lady Gray told host Ed Balls that briefings against her began while Labour was in opposition, when she "read about things that I would have said or not said," and continued once in government. She said she had tried to get Starmer's closest aide, Morgan McSweeney, "moved further away" from the prime minister, and that she had "always taken a view as a civil servant and actually as a special adviser that you should be able to speak freely in rooms." Asked whether Starmer should have acted on her concerns, she said: "I talked to him a couple of times about it. I was disappointed that not more was done." She described the atmosphere as "definitely unbearable, particularly for me. It was a very, very, very difficult period." In July, deputy leader Lucy Powell described a "boys' club" culture in No.10 that Andy Burnham would change.

### The reframe

Follow the sequence Gray describes and count the people who could, individually, defend their own part in it. She raised the briefings with Starmer directly, twice. He was told. She tried to have McSweeney moved further away; that did not happen, but the attempt was made and can be pointed to. Powell separately named a "boys' club" culture on the record. Each person involved has something they can say they did. None of it, on Gray's own account, changed anything for her.

This is not the usual shape of an accountability failure. There is no single moment where someone was told to look the other way. What Gray describes is closer to what happens when several people, each acting reasonably within their own role, produce between them an outcome nobody individually chose and nobody is straightforwardly responsible for. She spoke up. He listened, twice. The aide stayed where he was. "Unbearable" describes an atmosphere that persisted not because anyone refused to fix it, but because fixing it was never quite anyone's job in particular.

A cover-up can be exposed and ended. A structure in which everyone did something defensible, and the sum of those defensible actions was an unbearable workplace for the person at the centre of it, has no obvious point of failure to fix. It has a loop: raised, heard, attempted, unresolved. Gray left. The loop, on the available evidence, closed around her departure rather than around any change to how it operated.
