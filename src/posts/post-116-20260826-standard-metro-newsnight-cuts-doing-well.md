### The story

Newsnight is facing the axe, according to staff, with the BBC's flagship current affairs programme having shed jobs, seen its investigations unit merged with Sunday With Laura Kuenssberg, and been cut from an hour to 30 minutes. The show's team fell from 57 to 23 as part of 550 news job losses in June. A BBC source told the Sun that staff are worried about further budget reductions next year, describing it as "a worrying time." The corporation, which needs to save £500million over three years, insists Newsnight is doing so well that it is being moved to a peak-time slot on BBC Two on Fridays. It pointed to the show's 2million viewers and its history of holding the powerful to account, from Jeremy Paxman's interrogations to Emily Maitlis's questioning of Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor over his links to Jeffrey Epstein.

### The reframe

Two descriptions of the same programme are being offered at once. One is a staff account: a team cut by more than half, an investigations unit folded into another show, a running time halved, and a further round of cuts already flagged for next year. The other is the corporation's own account: a programme doing well enough to be promoted into a peak-time slot.

Both things can be true only if "doing well" is being measured by something other than the resources devoted to it. A programme built its reputation on long-form investigation and confrontational interviewing needs time, staff and editorial independence to do that work. Cutting the running time in half and merging the investigations unit into a different show does not obviously produce more of that work. What it produces is a schedule change that can be announced and a viewer figure that can be cited, both of which are available immediately, unlike the slower and harder-to-measure question of whether the programme still does what made it notable in the first place.

The BBC's own budget position, a mandated £500million saving over three years, is the more honest explanation sitting underneath the announcement. A promotion to peak time costs nothing beyond a scheduling decision. Reversing 550 job losses, or restoring an investigations unit that has already been merged away, would cost money the corporation says it does not have. When a resourcing problem cannot be solved, a scheduling one is offered instead, and described using the language, "doing so well," that would normally describe the thing that was actually cut.
