### The story

A major review led by Oxford University has found that vaping is a "viable option" for people who want to stop smoking, with e-cigarettes found to be less harmful than traditional cigarettes even though they are not risk-free. Drawing on data from 80 studies involving 30,000 people, the review found smokers using nicotine vapes were 61 per cent more likely to have quit after six months than those using gum or patches, and that vapes with nicotine outperformed those without it. Dr Nicola Lindson, who led the review, said quitting smoking is hard and people need a number of different options to keep trying. A colleague, Dr Jamie Hartmann-Boyce, added that unlicensed e-cigarettes remain potentially dangerous, citing unstable batteries and unregulated ingredients, but said the evidence for regulated nicotine vapes is now strong. Around 5.3million UK adults still smoke, and each year more than a third try to quit, according to the Office for National Statistics.

### The reframe

Nicotine, in this story, appears twice, once as the addictive substance a smoker is trying to escape, and once as the substance being recommended, in a different delivery mechanism, as the way to escape it. The review's finding is not that nicotine has become safe. It is that the packaging around it, patch, gum, cigarette, regulated vape, unlicensed vape, changes how the same underlying compound is classified, funded and recommended.

That sorting is doing real work here, and the review is careful about it: unlicensed vapes are flagged as genuinely dangerous, their batteries and ingredients unregulated in a way that gum and patches are not. But the more striking split is the one between smoking and regulated vaping, two nicotine products, one taxed and increasingly discouraged through decades of public health messaging, the other now recommended by an Oxford-led review as a route out of it. The harm has not disappeared in that handover. It has been re-administered, moved from a product the state has spent decades trying to eliminate to one it is now prepared to endorse as a lesser, licensed version of the same habit.

This is not a criticism of the science, which is measuring quit rates rather than making policy. It is a description of how a harm gets managed rather than removed. Cigarettes remain legal, taxed, and sold on every high street. Vapes, now recommended over gum and patches, are legal, taxed, and sold on the same high street. What has moved is not the presence of nicotine in British life but which shelf it is stocked on, and which of those shelves currently carries the state's endorsement.
