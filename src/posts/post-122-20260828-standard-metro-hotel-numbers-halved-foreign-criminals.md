### The story

The number of asylum seekers housed in hotels has halved in a year to 16,021, the lowest figure since 2022, official data has shown. Home secretary Shabana Mahmood said the figures mean the government is "restoring control at our border and fairness to the migration system." The overall asylum bill fell for the second quarter in a row, from £4.76billion to £4.36billion, with 73,068 people now in "non-hotel" accommodation such as shared homes, flats or converted military camps, a 3% rise. Despite this, the number of foreign criminals living in the community despite deportation orders jumped from 18,993 to 21,294. Migrants granted indefinite leave to remain hit 199,628, up 24% and the highest annual total for 16 years, with applications for citizenship also rising sharply ahead of tougher immigration rules due this autumn. Shadow justice secretary Nick Timothy blamed the government's new Sentencing Act, saying: "Foreign criminals living in the community should be deported. We warned there'd be grave consequences from the act, and here is yet another one rearing its head."

### The reframe

A halved hotel figure is the number that gets the headline and the home secretary's quote. A deportation backlog that grew by more than two thousand people in the same period is the number that gets a paragraph further down, attributed to a different piece of legislation, answered by a different politician, on a different day. Both numbers describe the same system, measured at the same time, and only one of them is being offered as evidence that the system is working.

This is the selective visibility the manuscript describes: not concealment, since every figure here is published, but a choice about which figure is allowed to carry the meaning. The hotel number is falling because people are being moved into flats, shared homes and converted military camps, a change of address rather than a change in how many people the system is processing or removing. The deportation number is rising because an order to leave the country has, for over twenty-one thousand people, produced no actual departure. One of these is framed as restored control. The other is framed as somebody else's legislation.

Nick Timothy's explanation, that the Sentencing Act is responsible, may or may not be accurate as a mechanism. What it demonstrates regardless is the pattern itself: an inconvenient number is assigned a cause external to the government answering for the convenient one, so that neither figure has to be reconciled with the other inside the same account. The asylum bill fell. The backlog it was supposed to be clearing did not. Both statements can be true, and the government's language ensures that only one of them gets called success.
