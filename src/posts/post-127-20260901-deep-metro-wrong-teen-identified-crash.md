### The story

Joshua Johnson, 19, was killed when the silver Toyota Corolla he was travelling in hit a tree early on 13 December near Rotherham. Driver Summer Louise Scott, 17, and one of her passengers also died at the scene. South Yorkshire Police identified the dead using visual checks rather than forensic tests, including driving licence photos, phones, bank cards and clothing descriptions, Doncaster coroner's court heard. Johnson's family were told he had died and spent hours at the hospital bedside of the other survivor, believing him to be their son. They only discovered the mistake when that boy, Trevor TJ Wynn, 17, woke from a coma three weeks later. Det Ch Insp Andy Knowles said police had now resolved identification "to a high degree of certainty" when asked whether the matter had been settled.

### The reframe, with the manuscript

Identity confirmation at a fatal collision is not usually presented to the public as a discretionary judgement call. It is presented as a procedure: the kind of thing that either happens correctly or does not happen at all. What Doncaster coroner's court heard was closer to an interpretive exercise, a licence photo, a phone, a clothing description, standing in for the forensic test that would have settled the question without room for error. The manuscript's account of how policing actually functions describes exactly this kind of substitution:

> *Officers are left to decide which rules matter, when they matter, and to whom they apply.*

Nobody wrote a rule saying that visual checks are an acceptable substitute for forensic identification at a fatal crash scene. Nobody needed to. The rule that governs in practice is whatever combination of available evidence a given set of officers judges sufficient in the moment, under whatever pressure of time, scene conditions and information available happens to apply that night. In most cases that judgement will be right, because the visual cues will point the same way the forensic result would have. This was one of the cases where they did not, and the family who paid for that gap were the ones with the least ability to detect it from the outside.

The manuscript's chapter on discretion is explicit that this kind of outcome does not require anyone to have acted badly:

> *Inconsistent outcomes do not require inconsistent individuals.*

No officer at the scene intended to misidentify a body. Each individual step, matching a phone to a name, a photo to a face, a description to a set of clothes, was a reasonable action taken by someone doing a difficult job under the worst possible conditions. What the manuscript describes is how these individually defensible steps can accumulate into an outcome that no single decision was responsible for, and that nobody could be shown to have caused through misconduct. The Johnson family were told their son was dead. They then spent hours, and went on to spend three weeks, at the bedside of a boy who was not their son, extending comfort and grief to a stranger's child while believing him to be their own. That did not happen because someone lied to them. It happened because a sequence of ordinary judgements, none of them individually wrong enough to name, was allowed to stand in for verification.

The manuscript's wider argument about where the cost of institutional shortcuts lands is also relevant here:

> *The burden of coherence is pushed downward.*

A coherent identification process is one where the same standard of proof applies regardless of how chaotic the scene, how limited the resources, or how much pressure exists to reach a conclusion quickly. Maintaining that coherence costs something: more time at the scene, more reliance on forensic teams, more delay before a family can be told anything at all. What happened instead was that the cost of an incoherent process, the gap between what a visual check can actually establish and what a family needs to be told with certainty, was pushed onto the people least equipped to absorb it. The Johnsons did not choose to be told the wrong thing. They inherited the consequence of a shortcut taken upstream, by a system that had every incentive to resolve identification quickly and comparatively little incentive to build in the friction that would have caught the error before it reached them.

Det Ch Insp Andy Knowles's statement that identification has now been resolved "to a high degree of certainty" is worth sitting with. It is a careful phrase, precise in what it does and does not claim, and it is the kind of language institutions reach for once an error has already been made public rather than before. Nothing in the coroner's court record suggests South Yorkshire Police set out to cut a corner. What the record does suggest is that the corner existed, that it was available to be cut under ordinary operational pressure, and that only a coma outlasting three weeks brought that fact into view. A system that relies on the improbable survival of a second victim to correct its own mistake is not describing a rare failure. It is describing what the process actually permits, most of the time without anyone noticing.
