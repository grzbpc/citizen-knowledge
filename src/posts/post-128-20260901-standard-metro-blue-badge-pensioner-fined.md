### The story

Retired police officer Roy Sanders, 73, was hit with £240 in parking fines at Russells Hall Hospital in Dudley, West Midlands, despite holding a valid blue badge, while taking his terminally ill wife Judy, 70, for treatment. Parking firm Nexus issued the fines after the car registration linked to the badge was recorded incorrectly, out by one character. Mr Sanders successfully appealed the charges but said the experience left him furious: "Every time, I was told to register the blue badge, but I had! I'm stuck in a circle of incompetence." Nexus has not disputed that the registration error was the cause.

### The reframe

A blue badge is supposed to work as a fixed credential: obtain it, display it, and the entitlement it confers no longer needs to be argued for at the point of use. What Mr Sanders encountered instead was a credential that only functioned if a separate, invisible database entry, the car's registration, matched it exactly. One wrong character in a field he never saw and had no way to check turned a valid badge into three separate £80 fines.

This is not a story about carelessness at Russells Hall Hospital, or about Mr Sanders failing to follow a process. He registered the badge, as required, more than once, each time being sent back into the same loop by staff who could only tell him to do the thing he had already done. The fault sat in a single mistyped field inside Nexus's own system, a fault invisible to the person it was charging and correctable only by someone with access to the database itself. The badge did not change. The car did not change. What changed, each time a fine was issued, was whether a private company's records happened to agree with the physical document in Mr Sanders's window.

The appeal succeeded, eventually, which is being offered here as evidence the system works. It is better read as evidence of what the system actually requires to work: a pensioner with the persistence and administrative literacy of a retired police officer, willing to formally contest three separate fines rather than pay them and move on. Most people are not equipped, or do not have the time while managing a terminally ill spouse's hospital appointments, to fight a "circle of incompetence" for its own sake. For them, the same one-character error produces a different outcome: not an overturned fine, but a paid one, silently absorbed as the cost of a system that presented itself as automatic and turned out to depend on a database field nobody was checking.
