### The story

Prison laundry rooms, offices and storage cupboards are to be turned into cells as Labour tries new ways of tackling the capacity crisis, in what has been a first major challenge for prime minister Andy Burnham, whose decision to reduce the number of offenders eligible for early release has added pressure to find more room in prisons. Justice secretary Alex Norris said this could be done by converting existing spaces such as storage or laundry rooms, adding: "None of this will be done at the expense of having a functioning day-to-day regime." The Ministry of Justice is also putting £110million towards speeding up construction of hundreds of new places, including a prison in Lancashire to hold 1,700. But Mark Fairhurst, national chairman of the Prison Officers' Association, said space was not the only issue: "Who's going to staff the new prisons? We can't retain staff. The workplace is too violent, the wages are too low and the retirement age is too high."

### The reframe

A laundry room converted into a cell is a capacity solution in the narrowest possible sense: it adds a space where a person can be held. It does nothing to address why the estate ran out of space in the first place, and the justice secretary's assurance, that none of this will come at the expense of "a functioning day-to-day regime," is a statement about appearance rather than about the underlying condition that produced the shortage.

The manuscript's account of enforcement is direct about what happens when a system built to punish reaches the edge of its own capacity: it is expanded rather than examined. "This machinery operates regardless of outcome." Storage cupboards becoming cells is what that expansion looks like in practice, a physical illustration of a system absorbing pressure by converting more of itself into holding space, rather than asking whether the number of people it is holding reflects a policy choice worth revisiting.

Mark Fairhurst's intervention points at the gap the announcement does not close. A converted laundry room is still a room. It is not a member of staff. The Prison Officers' Association's concern, that the workplace is too violent, the wages too low and the retirement age too high to retain the people needed to run an expanded estate, describes a capacity problem the government's £110million construction spend does not touch. New cells without new staff produce the same regime under more strain, not a functioning one under less. The laundry room did not create the crisis. Converting it does not resolve it either.
