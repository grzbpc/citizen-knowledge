### The story

A Royal College of Emergency Medicine poll of 2,126 A&E staff found that 96 per cent had experienced violence or aggression from patients or the public. Some 73 per cent said it happened daily, and two-thirds said the incidents involved discrimination against them. One consultant described being kicked in the face by a patient lying on a trolley. Only 4 per cent said they "always felt safe" at work, and of those who had reported an incident, only two in five said their NHS trust took meaningful action. Staff named long waiting times (90 per cent), overcrowding (85 per cent), and staff shortages and corridor care (both 65 per cent) as contributing factors. The RCEM president, Dr Ian Higginson, said: "We know long waits and crowding are key drivers of this, but that is not the fault of staff... that's on our governments." The college has launched a Safe to Care campaign. The Department of Health said any abuse of NHS staff was "unacceptable", adding that it had recently introduced standards all employers must follow to prevent attacks and keep staff safe.

### The reframe

The department's reply is accurate as far as it goes. Abuse of NHS staff is unacceptable, and standards for employers are a sensible thing to introduce. The difficulty is what the reply is answering. The people surveyed named the drivers themselves, and none of them is a standard: long waits, overcrowding, too few staff, patients on corridors. Dr Higginson placed the responsibility on governments. The department's response places it on employers.

Underneath that sits the figure that matters most. Of those who reported an incident, only two in five said their trust took meaningful action. A standard that every employer must follow is worth only as much as the trusts that follow it, and the poll is a record of what following looked like before the standards arrived. The department is entitled to say the standards are new and the survey may not yet measure them. That is a fair answer, and it is an answer about next year. The 96 per cent describes this one.

Dr Catherine Hayhurst of the college said she fears the violence has "become normalised". When 73 per cent of staff say something happens daily, it has stopped being an incident and become a working condition, and a working condition is the thing an announcement is least able to reach. The manuscript's account of announcement politics describes this arrangement: a response that can be issued immediately and reported as action, while the conditions that produced the problem remain in place beneath it. A standard can be published in a week. An emergency department that no longer fills beyond its capacity cannot.
