### The story

HMRC is planning to write to 1.32 million people explaining how to claim a £70 pension top-up. Women make up about 75 per cent of those eligible, roughly 990,000 people. The "low earner's pension payment" reimburses people who missed out on tax relief, and applies to people earning close to the £12,570 income tax threshold. Former pensions minister Steve Webb warned of a "real risk of huge non-take-up", saying: "Some may suspect it is a scam." The letters will go out until early next year.

### The reframe

The money is owed, and the person it is owed to has to ask for it. That is the first feature of this arrangement. The scheme exists because a group of low earners missed out on relief, and the correction depends on each of them reading a letter, believing it, and completing a claim. Nothing in the process is unreasonable on its own. Taken together, it places the work of putting right a gap in the system on the people the gap affected.

The second feature is the one Steve Webb names. The same page of the same paper carried a warning, from the Take Five to Stop Fraud campaign, that people should "put protection before politeness" and "hang up, shut the door or simply say no". Anyone who has taken that advice seriously will treat an unexpected letter about money from an official body with exactly the caution they have been told to show. Suspicion in this case is not a failing. It is the correct response to a public that has been trained to expect scams, meeting a genuine offer that looks like one.

It is also worth noticing who is on the list. Three quarters of the people eligible are women, and all of them earn close to the tax threshold, so this is a group for whom £70 is not trivial and who may have the least room to spend time working out whether a letter is genuine. The letters will take until early next year to arrive.

The arithmetic adds one more point. If every one of the 1.32 million claimed, the total paid out would be £92.4 million. That is the same figure the Take Five campaign gives for what scam calls stole last year. The scheme and the scam are of the same size, and the first depends on people not mistaking it for the second. The manuscript's account of safety nets is that access is conditional and the risk of misunderstanding sits with the claimant. Here the condition is belief, and each person who decides the letter is a scam leaves their share unclaimed.
