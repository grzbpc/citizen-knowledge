### The story

Police were warned about a mass anti-migration protest that shut down the Port of Dover days before it happened, an activist has claimed. Hundreds of masked demonstrators dressed in black blocked roads at the UK's busiest ferry port on Saturday; Kent Police said no arrests were made and that those involved had dispersed by midday. Maz Garside, a member of Stand Up To Racism, says she passed on details of a planned action to Counter Terrorism Policing through an online form four days earlier, and "No one got in contact. I heard nothing." Around 200 protesters brought traffic on the A20 to a standstill. Kent Police, criticised for a slow response, said the demonstration was "treated in line with national guidance as spontaneous". Chris Vinson, opposition leader on Dover Council, said: "Anyone who saw the planned, coordinated arrival of van-loads of men dressed in black, knows the police description is nonsense." Counter Terrorism Policing said: "Any reports we receive from the public are passed to officers, who assess the details and take action as appropriate."

Separately, an RNLI lifeboat carrying 140 migrants rescued in the Channel was refused entry to two ports. Southampton Vessel Traffic Service and King's Harbour Master Portsmouth declined permission to enter, according to a broadcast on an open marine VHF radio. The same message warned RNLI crew from Bembridge and Yarmouth on the Isle of Wight that failure to comply would lead to criminal prosecution. The crew instead landed the migrants at Eastney, where around 500 protesters confronted firefighters, police, Border Force and other officials. Associated British Ports said: "We had to decline the enquiry as the port did not have suitable reception facilities available." The Home Office said everyone brought ashore was being transported for processing "in the usual way".

### The reframe, with the manuscript

Two things happened on the same stretch of coast within days of each other, and the official language attached to each is worth reading side by side.

The first is the word "spontaneous". Kent Police have not said they had no warning. They have said that the protest was treated, in line with national guidance, as spontaneous. Counter Terrorism Policing has not said it received nothing from Maz Garside. It has said that reports from the public are passed to officers who assess them and take action as appropriate. Both statements describe a procedure. Neither describes what happened to this particular report, and Metro's account does not settle whether the form was read, by whom, or what was decided. The wording is built so that it does not have to be known.

The category does a good deal of work. An event classed as spontaneous is one nobody could have prepared for, so criticism of a slow response is answered by the label rather than by the facts. If the activist's account is right, the label is hard to square with it. If it is wrong, the label is a plain description. The manuscript is not concerned with which of those turns out to be true. It is concerned with what the system gains from the way it speaks:

> *As discretion expands, policing drifts away from preventing harm and toward managing disruption. The shift is subtle but consequential.*

The outcome reported on Saturday fits that description closely. The roads were closed, the roads were reopened by midday, nobody was arrested, and the matter is now one of whether the response was slow. Managing the disruption was achieved. Whether anything was prevented is a separate question that the statement does not address.

The second event is the lifeboat. Here the language is not procedural. It is exact: a named consequence, criminal prosecution, delivered over an open radio channel, in real time, to an identifiable crew from two named stations. The ports' stated reason, no suitable reception facilities, may be entirely sound as a matter of operations, and nothing in the report disputes it. The effect is still plain. The people who had brought 140 rescued people ashore were given an unambiguous warning of what would happen if they did not comply. At Portsmouth, Hampshire Police said that no arrests had been made when the protesters left, and that an investigation to identify offenders had been launched. The warning to the crew was given in advance. The investigation of the crowd came afterwards.

This is not a claim that the two cases are equivalent, or that anyone acted in bad faith. A lifeboat crew works inside a regulated system, answers to a port authority, and can be addressed on a radio channel. A crowd of masked men on a main road cannot be addressed in the same way. Those practical differences are real. What the manuscript describes is the pattern that results when they are left to discretion:

> *This is how discretion shifts from a practical tool to a form of power. When exercised within a coherent framework, discretion can humanise enforcement. When exercised within a contradictory one, it becomes arbitrary. People are left guessing which version of the law they will encounter, and when.*

A crew from the Isle of Wight knew which version of the law they would encounter. A member of the public who files a report through an online form does not, and neither does anyone watching a port blocked on a Saturday morning. The manuscript puts the strain on those who have to hold the system together:

> *Officers are asked to enforce rules they know cannot be applied universally while maintaining the appearance that those rules still mean something. They are expected to preserve order without exposing contradiction.*

"Spontaneous", "in line with national guidance" and "take action as appropriate" are all phrases that preserve order without exposing anything. They are accurate, and they end the conversation without answering it.
