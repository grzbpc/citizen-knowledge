### The story

A woman in her 80s was unable to buy food after her bank card was blocked, and then waited almost three weeks for a replacement. Bridget Fowkes, from New Romney in Kent, was left with no way to make payments and had no other cards. She could not withdraw cash at her local post office using a cheque because her bank, Lloyds, did not allow it at the time, although the service has since been reintroduced. Romney Marsh, which includes New Romney, Lydd, Dymchurch and Dungeness, lost its last bank four years ago. Ms Fowkes, who has arthritis, learned on 27 July that her card details had been compromised, when Lloyds sent a warning text. She believes they were obtained from a legitimate business she had used. A relative eventually paid for her groceries until the replacement arrived. She credited the Lloyds fraud team for acting swiftly but complained about the delay: "I couldn't buy food because I couldn't access my money. This shows a wider problem of a lack of banking provision on Romney Marsh. Banks here have closed their branches down. We've got nothing for miles. I think they're relying too much on technology." Lloyds declined to comment.

### The reframe

The control worked. Lloyds sent a warning and the card was blocked. Ms Fowkes herself credits the fraud team for acting quickly. What failed was everything that was meant to sit behind it.

A blocked card is a sensible safeguard in a system where payment is expected to be electronic, because it assumes there is somewhere else to go. A branch to ask. A cheque that can be cashed at the post office. Cash in the house. In this case the branch had gone four years earlier, the cheque route was not permitted by her bank at the time, and she had no other card. Each of the alternatives was available in principle and none was available to her. The result was a woman unable to buy food for the length of time it takes to post a replacement card.

The manuscript makes its case about cash in terms of privacy. It describes cash as the route people imagine they can fall back on in a digital system, and argues that in a mostly cashless environment the fallback is less available, and less innocent, than it looks. Ms Fowkes' story is the practical version. She was not trying to avoid being recorded. She needed to buy food. The nearest branch had closed, the post office route was shut to her by her own bank's rules, and the one channel she did use had been stopped by an institution that was acting properly.

It is also a reminder of what the protective measure assumes. A fraud system treats a card as the person. When the card is stopped, the person is stopped too, and the cost of the protection falls on the customer for as long as the replacement takes. A relative stepped in. A woman in her 80s without one would have been in a different position, and Ms Fowkes' suggestion that the bank's directors try living somewhere with no banks, and £20 in cash each, is a sharper way of saying the same thing.
