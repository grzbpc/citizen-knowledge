### The story

Britain's air traffic control chief must be fired after hundreds of flights were grounded for a second day, airline chiefs have said. Martin Rolfe was in charge of National Air Traffic Services (NATS) in 2023 for a similar meltdown, when a garbled flight plan crippled the computer system and an IT engineer working at home could not log in and had to travel in to reboot it; days of chaos affected 700,000 passengers. On Tuesday a four-hour outage hit one in three flights. On Wednesday thousands were still stuck at airports including Gatwick and Heathrow, and 327 more arrivals and departures were cancelled. Over two days, 1,000 flights were axed, 1,000 were delayed and 330,000 travellers were affected, according to the operators' group Airlines UK. Ryanair chief Neal McMahon said "exactly the same issue" was to blame. Wizz Air UK's chief, Yvonne Moynihan, said this was the third failure since 2023 and that it was time to "consider three strikes and you're out". British Airways said it was "tired of dealing with these issues, time after time".

Mr Rolfe said NATS has "one of the best reputations in the world" for safety and resilience. He said: "Inevitably, things go wrong in complex systems", adding that it was "disappointing and upsetting, for which I apologise". He went on: "I don't believe we've ever seen an incident happen more than once, so this will be something different that we've never seen in 50 years. We had everybody on site, everybody was in the right place. We had all the engineers in place, so this was resolved as quickly as it could be, within the limits of making sure it all stayed safe." Asked if he would quit, he said what he took responsibility for was "keeping people safe and delivering the best service we can". Downing Street said he has "the confidence of the government" and that he had been ordered to give the transport secretary, Heidi Alexander, the results of a "full investigation" next week. Ms Alexander told MPs: "I would like to apologise to passengers." Having spoken to Mr Rolfe, she said, "while my understanding is we can rule out a cyber attack... I do not believe this issue was unavoidable". One couple were approached by a fake British Airways employee trying to sell them two £600 flights to Lisbon from another airport; their bank told them not to go ahead.

### The reframe, with the manuscript

Every statement made on Wednesday can be defended. The chief executive apologised. He said the engineers were in place and the incident was resolved as quickly as safety allowed. The Government said it had confidence in him and that an investigation would report within a week. The transport secretary apologised, said she did not think the failure was unavoidable, and said a cyber attack could be ruled out. Nobody has said anything that is wrong on its face.

That is the feature the manuscript identifies in what it calls the professional loop. Responsibility in such a loop does not disappear. It is distributed:

> *When the project underperforms, responsibility is distributed rather than owned. Failure is attributed to market conditions, implementation challenges, or unforeseeable complexity. The process itself remains unquestioned.*

"Inevitably, things go wrong in complex systems" is a sentence of exactly that kind. It is probably true. It is also a sentence that moves a failure from the category of something someone did to the category of something that happens. A later sentence in the chief executive's answer works in the same way from the other direction: "I don't believe we've ever seen an incident happen more than once." Wizz Air's chief counts this as the third failure since 2023, and the paper's own account of 2023 describes a similar meltdown that year. Whether the two events are the same, as Ryanair says, or different, as NATS says, is a question the investigation may answer. What the exchange shows is that the definition of "the same incident" is being asserted by the organisation whose record depends on the answer.

The account of what happened on the day follows the same shape. "We had everybody on site, everybody was in the right place." This is the language of a procedure that was performed correctly. The manuscript has a precise description of why that is not a reassurance:

> *Each professional group can demonstrate that it fulfilled its role correctly. Each approval is defensible in isolation.*

The passage is written about projects that are approved at every stage and fail anyway. An outage is not a project, and I do not claim that NATS reviewed its way into this fault. The relevance is to the response. The apology, the staffing on the day, the confidence of the Government and the promise of an investigation are each correct steps, and together they make a sequence that can be completed without anyone being asked the question the airlines are asking.

The airlines are the only voices in the report that speak from outside the loop. Wizz Air's "three strikes" and British Airways' "tired of dealing with these issues, time after time" are not procedural statements. They are counts, kept by the customers, of how many times the same thing has happened. The manuscript's name for what is missing is an anchor:

> *What is missing is not diligence, but an anchor outside the loop against which success or failure can be meaningfully judged.*

For a service that took one in three flights down for four hours on a Tuesday and was still cancelling flights on Wednesday, the anchor is not hard to find. It is 330,000 people on the floor of Heathrow, and a record of three failures since 2023 against which the apology is being measured. The investigation is due next week. The manuscript's question is whether anything outside the loop will be allowed to judge what it finds.
