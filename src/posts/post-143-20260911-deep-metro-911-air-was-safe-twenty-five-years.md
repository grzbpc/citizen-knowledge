### The story

Twenty-five years after the attacks that killed 2,977 people, the legacy of 9/11 continues as cancers claim the lives of thousands who breathed in its clouds of toxic dust. Within two weeks, with Ground Zero still a sea of twisted metal, 300,000 office workers, 50,000 students and teachers and 35,000 residents returned to the area. "Trade Center Cough" was rife. New York lawyer Michael Barasch, who was a few blocks away on the day, said: "We naively thought our government would never lie to us. Since then tens of thousands have developed respiratory illnesses. Over 52,000 people have been certified with a 9/11 cancer, over 9,000 people have died of World Trade-related pulmonary illnesses and cancers, and it does not stop."

Former NYPD officer Harold Delaney, who was at Ground Zero within hours, said: "I knew on my first day at Ground Zero it should have been a hazmat area." He later developed chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and then prostate cancer. Frances Caulfield, now 61, returned to work two weeks after the attack. "Even though the air was horrible and smelled weird and chemical-like, I figured they'd tested it, and it was harmless," she said. She was later diagnosed with breast cancer. Dana Cohen, a senior partner at the firm Barasch & McGarry, which represents 9/11 illness victims, said clients told her: "They told us the air was safe", and "we wanted to believe it".

New York's mayor, Zohran Mamdani, this week released more than 170,000 documents showing that asbestos was found "nearly everywhere" after the attacks. "People got sick because leaders they trusted lied and told them they were safe to breathe in toxic air," he said. One file shows that a former deputy mayor, Robert Harding, warned the city "could face as many as 35,000 legal claims for its response to the attacks". Victims have faced several barriers. At first, only those who went to hospital within 72 hours could get redress. Mr Barasch also fought to have a compensation scheme extended to 2090.

### The reframe, with the manuscript

The question in 2001 was whether it was safe to go back. Nobody could answer it on the day, because the answer depended on testing that had not been done. What was available was a reassuring statement, and on the evidence in Metro's report that statement was the thing people acted on. Frances Caulfield did not say she had been told the air was tested. She said she "figured they'd tested it". The assumption is the telling part. People believed in a check because the language of the institution implied that one had taken place.

The manuscript describes the pressure behind this kind of statement without suggesting that the people who make it are lying in the ordinary sense:

> *Governments must still appear responsive. Public bodies must still appear competent. Political leaders must still reassure citizens that problems are understood and that action is being taken. Language expands to fill the gap.*

Mr Mamdani's word for what happened is that leaders lied. That is his claim, made about the city's leaders at the time, and it rests on documents the paper reports but does not itself review. The manuscript's argument does not depend on it. A reassurance given when the evidence is incomplete does the same damage whether it is intended as a lie or as comfort, and the 170,000 documents are only now in the public domain, a quarter of a century after the people who relied on the statement had returned to their offices and schools.

The second thing the report records is how redress was built. At first, only those who went to hospital within 72 hours could claim. A condition that cannot be seen for years was measured against a window of three days. Mr Barasch had to fight to have the compensation scheme extended to 2090, because the illnesses outlast every date the original arrangements assumed. This is not an accusation of bad faith either. It is what a system looks like when it is designed around the event that has just happened rather than the consequence that is coming.

The manuscript is clear about what correction requires, and about why it is slow:

> *Correction requires acknowledgement. Acknowledgement requires honesty. And honesty requires institutions to admit that some of the performances intended to protect legitimacy have become obstacles to restoring it.*

Releasing the files is an act of acknowledgement, whatever one thinks of the politics around it. The cost of its being so late is carried by the people in the report: Harold Delaney, who avoids the news for two weeks every September, and the families who still ring the lawyers' office to report another death.

The cycle the manuscript names is the one that matters most for anyone reading about 9/11 now:

> *The less trust institutions possess, the more heavily they rely upon symbolic reassurance. The more heavily they rely upon symbolic reassurance, the more obvious the gap between language and experience becomes.*

Dana Cohen's clients said they wanted to believe it. That sentence is what the arrangement runs on. People want to believe an official statement, and the willingness to believe it is the resource that gets spent. Twenty-five years later, the figure that Mr Barasch gives, more than 9,000 dead of the illnesses against 2,977 on the day, is the measure of what it cost.
