### The story

Five hundred fraud-busters are to be hired in a £500 million crackdown on money laundering at Post Offices and High Street shops, the Government says. They will hunt organised crime gangs who use them to channel profits abroad, including to Russia. Post Offices have been identified as a "key vector" for sending dirty money, and also linked to "hostile state actors" operating in the UK. Since 2017, branches have been able to accept cash deposits on behalf of banks, but with less rigorous checks: a customer hands over banknotes to be counted, then inserts a bank card and enters the PIN. The Anti-Money Laundering and Asset Recovery Strategy was unveiled by home secretary Shabana Mahmood and security minister Dan Jarvis. "The fight against organised crime does not stop at arresting offenders," Mr Jarvis said. "We must also go after dirty money that fuels their operations by strengthening our ability to trace, seize and recover criminal assets." The officers will be deployed across police forces, the National Crime Agency and the Crown Prosecution Service, and the NCA will launch a Financial Intelligence Service to improve co-operation with banks.

Part of the funding will come from £350 million stripped from criminals in the past year. The Government says it stopped £1 billion of dirty money being moved, disrupted 2,700 illicit finance operations and returned £26 million to victims, as laundering convictions hit 4,000. The NCA has estimated that at least £12 billion of criminal cash is generated and laundered in the UK each year. Barber shops were thought to be the hub, but officers have found a wide range of stores involved, from petrol stations to vape shops. The focus has now turned to what is described as the "big problem" of Post Offices. Ministers say that as well as having fewer security checks than banks, Post Office staff have less training and so are less likely to recognise laundering patterns. As banks close branches, Post Offices become more convenient to gangs, and counters often have poor CCTV. A Post Office spokesperson said: "Money laundering is a complex issue which affects organisations across the financial sector. We continue to invest in controls, training and partnerships across the industry so we can respond to evolving threats."

### The reframe, with the manuscript

The report describes a hole, and it describes how the hole came to be. Since 2017, Post Office branches have taken cash deposits on behalf of banks with less rigorous checks. Banks have since closed branches, which has made the Post Office counter the more convenient option. The staff, ministers say, have less training. The counters often have poor CCTV. Each of those facts is an arrangement that someone made, or allowed, and they add up to a channel through which criminal money was easy to move. The response is 500 new officers and £500 million.

The manuscript asks one question of enforcement on this scale, and the report gives some of the figures needed to start answering it:

> *What is far less often examined is whether the cost of enforcement corresponds meaningfully to the harm it claims to address.*

The harm is estimated by the NCA at no less than £12 billion a year. The Government says £350 million was stripped from criminals in the past year and £26 million was returned to victims. The two sets of figures measure different things, and the ratio between them is not a success rate. They do give a sense of scale: £350 million is under 3 per cent of the NCA's estimate, and £26 million is about a fifth of 1 per cent. The figures the Government offers are also of a different kind. Money stopped, operations disrupted and convictions achieved are counts of activity. The amount of money laundered in Britain, which is the thing the strategy is meant to reduce, is not reported as having fallen.

The manuscript is clear about why activity counts are the ones that get reported:

> *Once enforcement systems reach a certain scale, they develop self-preserving dynamics. Jobs depend on continued activity. Budgets depend on demonstrable workload. Performance metrics reward throughput rather than resolution. Success is measured by arrests, charges, seizures, or compliance actions, not by sustained reductions in harm.*

None of this implies bad faith. Taking £350 million from criminals is a real outcome, and 4,000 convictions are 4,000 people. The point in the manuscript is structural. A new unit with 500 officers, a new Financial Intelligence Service and a new strategy is each a new institution with a budget that depends on the workload it can show. The measures that will be used to judge it are, in the nature of the thing, the ones it can produce.

There is a second question that the strategy does not appear to ask. It targets the places where money is laundered. It does not, on this report, revisit the arrangement of 2017 that made one of those places easy to use, or the closure of bank branches that made it more convenient. The Post Office's own statement says it will keep investing in controls and training. The ministers' description of the problem is that controls and training were weaker than at banks. That is an admission that the channel operates with fewer protections than a bank does, and the plan is to add them after the arrangement has been in place for nine years.

The manuscript names the test that follows:

> *A system that demands compliance must justify its costs as well as its rules.*

The strategy will need to show, in a year or five, that less money is being laundered in Britain than the £12 billion the NCA now estimates. That would be a different kind of figure from the ones in this report, and it would be the first one that measures the harm rather than the effort.
