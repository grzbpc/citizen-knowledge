### The story

Democracy activists say they feel "betrayed" after being blocked from coming to the UK from Hong Kong over convictions for rallying against China, which they describe as the work of a "kangaroo court". Hong Kongers were promised a legal route to the UK in 2020 amid increasing crackdowns from Chinese authorities. Two democracy protesters told Metro the Home Office denied them British National Overseas (BNO) visas in July because convictions for "riot" were taken at face value. Lawyers told Metro that at least 10 other activists had been denied BNO visas for similar reasons since Andy Burnham became prime minister. Priti Patel, who announced the scheme as home secretary in 2020, said: "Burnham and Labour are kowtowing to China. We need to protect and safeguard Hong Kongers who are having their freedoms undermined and suppressed."

The two men, John and Edward, are not their real names. Edward was arrested at a pro-democracy protest in 2019 as a student, says he was beaten before being charged with riot, pleaded not guilty and was sentenced to three and a half years in January 2022. He applied for a BNO visa in October 2025 and was refused in July. John was among two thousand activists who clashed with police on 19 November 2019, pleaded guilty to riot in the hope of a shorter sentence, and served more than two years before being released in November 2025. He applied after being threatened by police for refusing to name fellow activists.

Under British immigration law, as Metro reports it, an application must be refused when the applicant has received a sentence longer than 12 months, but that does not apply for a crime "that would not be criminal in the UK". Edward argued this applied to his conviction for riot. The Home Office disagreed. The refusal letter quoted extensively from the Hong Kong court judgment, with UK officials ruling that his conduct would be criminal in the UK. John's refusal came a day after Edward's and followed almost identical wording. Their lawyer, Kate Lam, called the refusals a "betrayal of the main purpose of the BNO policy". More than 170,000 people have used the BNO route since it was introduced. Bobby Dean MP, of the All-Party Parliamentary Group on Hong Kong, fears promises are being "walked back on". The Home Office said it could not "assess the veracity" of "these anonymised claims", and that the Government "remains steadfast in its support for members of the Hong Kong community in the UK and is fully committed to the BNO route".

### The reframe, with the manuscript

Nothing in the report shows the Home Office acting outside the law, and this entry does not claim it. As Metro describes it, the rule is that an application must be refused where the sentence exceeds 12 months, unless the offence would not be criminal in the UK. Both men were sentenced to more than 12 months. The question is the exception, and the exception turns on one judgement: whether what a Hong Kong court found they had done would be a crime in Britain.

That judgement is made by officials, working from the Hong Kong judgments themselves. The refusal letter, the report says, quoted extensively from the court's findings. The applicants' case is that those findings are not a reliable account, because the convictions came from what the activists call a "kangaroo court". Metro cannot say who is right, and neither can this entry. What the report does show is where the weight of the decision sits. The evidence described is the judgment, and the judgment is the thing the applicants dispute.

The manuscript describes how a threshold of this kind operates, whether or not anyone is acting in bad faith:

> *This ambiguity is not incidental. Clear thresholds would constrain discretion. Opaque ones expand it. When the point of activation is uncertain, enforcement can be justified after the fact and framed as inevitable rather than chosen.*

The exception to the 12-month rule is a threshold with an edge that the report states in a single phrase, "would not be criminal in the UK". Whether a riot conviction from 2019 falls on one side of it depends on how a Hong Kong judgment is read, and nobody outside the department can see how it was read except through the letters. Those letters, as described, use almost identical wording a day apart. That may only mean a standard template, which is what a department handling a large volume of cases would be expected to use. It also means that the reasoning on show is the same for two men with different histories: one who pleaded not guilty and says he was beaten, and one who pleaded guilty hoping for a shorter sentence and says he was pressured by police to name others.

The manuscript is exact about how a decision of this kind is presented:

> *This framing is persuasive because it feels orderly. The rule existed. The behaviour crossed a line. The response followed.*

The Home Office statement follows that form. It says it cannot "assess the veracity" of "these anonymised claims", and that the Government is "fully committed to the BNO route". The first is a reasonable position when the applicants are anonymous. It also means the claims cannot be tested in public, and that the commitment can be restated without being measured against these cases. What disappears from view, in the manuscript's words, is the discretionary space in between: the point at which an official decides that a conviction abroad is a crime here.

The political claims in the report are for others. Priti Patel says the Government is kowtowing to China. Bobby Dean fears promises are being walked back. Both are arguments about intention. The narrower question the report raises is about procedure, and it can be asked of any government: when a rule says "must refuse" and then carves out an exception, who can see the exception being applied? More than 170,000 people have used the BNO route, which shows the scheme works for a great many. The lawyers say at least ten have been refused. Nobody has said how many applications turned on this exception, or how many of those succeeded, and without that figure there is no way to tell whether the two men in this report are unusual or ordinary.
