### The story

Britain's air traffic control system was in crisis again after thousands of passengers were grounded by a computer glitch for the second time in two weeks. Airline chiefs demanded the Government fire National Air Traffic Services (NATS) chief executive Martin Rolfe, who is already under fire over three major meltdowns in five years, including one that hit 2,000 flights earlier this month. Transport secretary Heidi Alexander, who gave Mr Rolfe a week to explain what went wrong after the earlier failure, called the new outage "deeply frustrating" for passengers. The Prime Minister's spokesperson did not back him, saying only that Ms Alexander's review was "still ongoing". Mr Rolfe has held the £1.4 million-a-year post since 2015. The glitch at Prestwick, Scotland, spread to Glasgow, Manchester, London City, Luton and Belfast airports. NATS said the "connectivity issue" was "unrelated to the issue two weeks ago" and that it was fixed quickly. Ryanair said "Enough is enough" as 25,000 of its passengers on 140 flights were hit.

### The reframe

NATS may well be right that the two faults are unrelated. A connectivity problem at one centre and a failure two weeks earlier can have different causes, and an engineer would say so. The word is accurate. It also does something else.

By calling the second outage unrelated to the first, the operator removes it from a series. Each fault is then an individual event with its own cause, its own fix and its own apology. The airlines count differently. Ryanair is counting passengers delayed, and the report puts the three major failures in five years in a single line. The unit of measurement is different, and the dispute between them is mostly a dispute about which unit counts. An operator counts faults. A passenger counts how often they have been stranded.

The manuscript describes why this kind of dispute is hard to resolve. Complexity becomes a form of insulation. Outsiders are told that the system is too technical to understand, and insiders are rewarded for keeping it running. Nobody in that position is lying. The transport secretary has asked for a review, and it is still under way. The Prime Minister's spokesperson has said so. The transport secretary has called the outage deeply frustrating. Every step is proper, and the result is a long sequence of proper steps that has not yet produced an answer to the question the airlines are asking, which is whether the thing is going to keep happening.

The report mentions the salary, and the airlines have made their case about the person. This entry does not take up that argument. The question that does not depend on any individual is who is responsible for the system as a whole, as distinct from each fault. If every failure is unrelated, the answer is that nobody is responsible for the pattern, since the pattern does not exist. That would be an uncomfortable conclusion for an operator whose customers have just counted three.
