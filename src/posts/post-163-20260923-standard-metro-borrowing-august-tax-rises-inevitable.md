### The story

Government borrowing rose in August to the second-highest level for the month on record, coming in above forecasts and piling pressure on chancellor John Healey ahead of his first autumn Budget. Public sector net borrowing hit £18.3 billion, some £3.5 billion more than official forecasters expected and 19% up on last year, the Office for National Statistics said. Only August 2020 was higher. Debt interest payments reached £8.8 billion, the highest August figure on record, driven by inflation pushing up returns on index-linked gilts. Since April the Government has borrowed £77.3 billion, £8.1 billion above the Office for Budget Responsibility's March forecast. Thomas Pugh, chief economist at RSM UK, said: "The jump in borrowing sets the stage for a much trickier Budget. Another round of tax rises in October now looks inevitable." He estimated Mr Healey's headroom at £10 billion to £15 billion, below the £24 billion left by Rachel Reeves.

### The reframe

Most households will read this as a number about the country. It is also a number about a mechanism most households were never shown. Debt interest reached a record for August because inflation raised the returns on index-linked gilts. A rise in prices, which people feel at the till, appears in the public accounts as a rise in what the state owes, and the state's answer, if the economist is right, will come back to the same people as tax.

None of that is hidden. It is reported in the same paragraph. The difficulty is that it is reported in a language that asks the reader to accept it. Borrowing was £3.5 billion above what forecasters expected, which means the forecasters were wrong by a sum that sounds enormous and is, in the context of the whole budget, a modest miss. The economist says another round of tax rises looks inevitable, and the headroom is described as £10 billion to £15 billion, down from £24 billion. Each figure is precise. None of them tells an ordinary reader what to do with it, or what would have been different if the forecast had been right.

The manuscript describes this as a distinct kind of legitimacy problem. People are required to take part, through pensions, savings and tax, while understanding is treated as optional. They are told not to worry if they cannot explain how it works. The professionals who do explain it, the economists and forecasters, are reporting from inside the same structure and, in this case, have just been wrong by billions. That does not make them dishonest. It makes the explanation a moving target.

The question this entry leaves is a small one. When a Budget is described in advance as inevitable, what part of it is still a decision? An economist's estimate of the headroom is an estimate. The Budget is a choice, and the word "inevitable" is a prediction that, by being repeated, starts to behave like an instruction.
