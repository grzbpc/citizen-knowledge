### The story

Nearly £5 million has been paid out to people who were wrongly convicted of being fare dodgers, but thousands may not know they are owed. More than 48,000 people had their criminal convictions overturned after a 2024 ruling that train companies had spent years unlawfully using the fast-track Single Justice Procedure (SJP) to prosecute alleged fare dodging. The procedure allows magistrates to convict and sentence in private. Letters went out to all the people who had been wrongly convicted, and newly released Ministry of Justice data shows £4,900,096 in fines, costs and court fees has been repaid so far. But more than half of the people who paid their fines have not yet come forward, with £2,603,463 waiting to be repaid, and thousands may not know their convictions have been overturned.

The figures came out through a Freedom of Information Act request, and the Ministry of Justice has been accused of not doing enough to track down all those affected. Penelope Gibbs, director of the campaign group Transform Justice, which obtained the data, said: "The biggest miscarriage of justice in English and Welsh history has been compounded by the unwillingness of the state to find and compensate victims." She said the state should make every effort to ensure people know their conviction is null and void, and that the average person paid £319 in fines and costs. The Ministry of Justice said it is trying to contact everyone who was wrongly convicted between 2018 and 2023, but did not elaborate on the steps being taken.

### The reframe, with the manuscript

The article gives two speeds. The convictions were produced in bulk, in private, through a fast-track procedure, over years. The reversal is being done by letter, and it depends on the person who was wrongly convicted reading it, understanding it and applying for the money. More than half of those who paid have not done so. Nobody in the report says the letters were not sent. The report says they have not been enough.

Ms Gibbs's description of the case is hers, and it is a strong one. The Ministry's position is that it is trying to contact everyone, though it did not say how. This entry does not decide between them. It takes the figures as given: about £4.9 million repaid, about £2.6 million waiting, and a procedure the courts found had been used unlawfully.

The manuscript makes a short point about cost, in a passage on enforcement that absorbs resources without achieving its aims.

> *The financial cost is measurable. The opportunity cost is not.*

In this case the financial cost is unusually easy to count: £4.9 million repaid, £2.6 million waiting, an average of £319 per person. Each conviction was a demand for compliance, backed by the authority of a court. What the report cannot count is the other kind of cost, to the people who carried a criminal record they should not have had, some of whom may still be carrying it without knowing it has gone.

The manuscript is careful about where the argument stops.

> *The issue is not whether enforcement should exist. It is whether its financial, social, and moral costs remain aligned with its outcomes.*

Nobody in this story is arguing that fare evasion should go unpunished. The argument is about the route. A procedure that is fast and private produces its outcomes faster than it can correct them, and once a ruling has said the outcomes were unlawful, the correction becomes a second piece of work that has to be funded, staffed and chased. That work has no urgency built into it, because the people it concerns are not in a position to demand it. They do not know they are owed anything.

The manuscript names the point at which this becomes a question of legitimacy.

> *When that alignment fails, enforcement no longer signals responsibility. It signals inertia.*

The word "inertia" needs care. It does not mean that anyone is refusing to act. The Ministry says it is acting. It describes a system that keeps moving in the direction it was already moving, and in which the work of reversing a mistake is slower than the work that made it. Whether that describes the repayment scheme is something the Ministry's own figures, when they are next published, will help to show.
