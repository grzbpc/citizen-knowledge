### The story

Long-term jobless benefit claimants could be banned from spending welfare payments on alcohol, cigarettes and gambling under plans launched by the Conservatives. The proposal would also cut universal credit for those who have not made consistent contributions to the welfare system, which Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch said was currently "unfair to workers". Under the plan, anyone who claims universal credit when out of work would get six months of full benefits to find a job. After that, the full rate would only be available to people who have paid into the system through taxes for years. Those who have not would have their standard rate cut by a third, and the payment would be loaded onto a "back to work" pre-payment card that would ban spending on cigarettes, alcohol, betting and cash withdrawals. Mrs Badenoch said: "What is unfair is a system where someone who can work chooses not to and earns more from benefits than someone in a job."

### The reframe

This is a proposal from one party, set out ahead of any election, and this entry is not a judgement on its merits. Supporters would say that a benefit system should be fair to the people who pay for it, that a contribution record is a reasonable test, and that a card restricting certain purchases keeps public money for the purposes it was given for. Those are real arguments and many voters hold them.

What the manuscript looks at is a narrower thing: where the scrutiny falls. In a society that moralises work, employment stands for character. Those in work are assumed to be contributing, whatever the work is. Those outside it have to show why they should be supported, and the proof asked of them is detailed. The manuscript's phrase for this is that proof replaces trust.

The proposal can be read in that light without any bad intent attached to it. The test is a history of tax paid. The restriction is a list of things that cannot be bought, and cash is on it, so the claimant's spending would be visible in a way that a worker's is not. Nobody asks an employed person which shops they may use. The six months of full benefit, after which the rate changes, sets out the moral timetable plainly: a period of presumed good faith, then a record to be shown.

The statement that the system currently lets someone earn more on benefits than in work is a factual claim about incomes, and the report does not give figures for it. That claim is the part of the argument that can be checked, and it is the part a reader could usefully ask to see.
