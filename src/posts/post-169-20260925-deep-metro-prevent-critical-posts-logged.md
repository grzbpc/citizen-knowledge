### The story

Social media posts criticising government policy are being monitored and investigated by officials, Metro can reveal. X and Reddit posts from Britons attacking the UK's anti-terror programme Prevent were added to a database of online comments. Metro obtained a list of 77 critical posts found and logged by the Standards and Compliance Unit (StaCU), which monitors Prevent, between March 2024 and February 2025. Jacob Smith of Rights and Security International, who fought for a year to get the Home Office to publish the information, said: "You should be allowed to criticise government policy without being put on a list."

StaCU was set up in 2024 to investigate complaints about Prevent, the safeguarding programme designed to stop people becoming terrorists. As well as processing reports made directly to it, officials appear to be actively scouring the internet for critical posts. A Freedom of Information request revealed the log. Many of the posts came from ordinary people who would not suspect officials were looking at their feeds. A number claim Prevent is "biased" in how it trains public sector staff to spot radicalisation. The charity Campaign Against Antisemitism had a post logged after it criticised policing at a Hezbollah vigil, saying: "We aren't sure whether to be flattered or concerned." The digital rights group Open Rights had a post logged which said Prevent "lacks transparency" over what data it holds.

StaCU says it attempts to verify claims about Prevent independently, which it managed for only 10% of posts. Its parent body, the Commission for Countering Extremism, publicly replies to some, asking users to submit a formal complaint, but does not tell them their post has already been logged. Mr Smith submitted his request in July 2025 but the data was only released after an appeal to the Information Commissioner's Office. He said: "Whilst these may be social media posts, these are not people who posted thinking the government might be looking at their feed." The Home Office said: "Prevent does not track people who may have criticised the programme, nor does it target any particular community. Keeping the public safe is our number one priority. We are committed to ensuring everyone is protected from radicalisation."

### The reframe, with the manuscript

There are two statements in the report that can both be true. The Home Office says Prevent does not track people who may have criticised it. The log records 77 critical posts. A post is not a person, and a log of posts is not necessarily a list of people. The report does not say whether account names were recorded, and that is the detail on which the two statements would turn. This entry takes no view on it.

The unit has a clear job, which is to look into complaints about a programme, and there is nothing odd in an organisation noticing public criticism of what it oversees. The question the report raises is about the people who wrote the posts. They wrote them in public, to an audience they assumed was other members of the public. That assumption used to be safe for a reason the manuscript explains.

> *The difficulty of connection acted as a form of privacy.*

A post on a public forum was always readable by anyone. What protected the writer was that nobody was likely to read it, still less collect it with others, still less file it by topic. A statement could be public in the technical sense and obscure in practice. The manuscript calls that practical obscurity, and says it rested on friction and not on secrecy.

What has changed is how cheap the assembly has become. The report describes officials who "appear to be actively scouring" the internet, which means that the collecting no longer waits for a complaint. The manuscript describes what happened to the friction.

> *The friction that once protected ordinary life has been reduced by the same systems that make ordinary life more convenient.*

The same convenience that lets a person post to thousands of strangers in a minute lets a unit search those posts by keyword in a minute. Nobody had to ask the writers anything. The posts were already there.

The detail that most affects the reader is the one about replies. The Commission for Countering Extremism sometimes answers a critical post in public and asks the person to make a formal complaint, and does not say that the post has already been logged. Each step is proper. The reply is polite and the invitation to complain is a real one. The writer is in the position of being read by a system without being told.

None of this establishes that anyone has been harmed, and the report does not claim it. The Home Office says no one has been tracked. What the log shows is smaller and, for that reason, easier to take in: a person who criticises a government programme online can now be read, counted and filed by the body that oversees it, and will probably find out only if someone files a request, waits a year and appeals.
