### The story

Britain's first retail facial recognition system has launched in a coffee shop, but campaigners say "pay with a glance" endangers civil liberties and risks unleashing fraud on a dangerous new level. Customers tap "pay with smile" at a terminal, then look into a screen as if unlocking a smartphone. The system, run by the online bank Revolut, checks their biometrics against those taken when they first signed up. It began offering the option for free to its customers at Kiss The Hippo cafes in Bloomsbury, Chelsea and Soho. Revolut's Alex Codina said it replaces outdated, fragmented tills with "a hyper-efficient checkout experience". The chain's managing director, Alex Damgaci, said it will be rolled out to "all 12 of our cafes later this year". Revolut said the checkout will help customers whose "hands are full" or who cannot find their card, and insisted personal data is "encrypted end-to-end" and not kept by merchants.

Jack Coulson, head of advocacy at the civil liberties group Big Brother Watch, warned people "should be under no illusions that giving away their biometrics is safe". He said Revolut had this month given a fraudster the home addresses, passport details and face data of some users, a claim that is his and that the report does not test. Professor Btijah Ajana of King's College London said the technology "normalises surveillance in the name of convenience and security, making compliance passive and opting out increasingly difficult", and that "you cannot change your face the way you change your password". Big Brother Watch wants dedicated legislation for facial recognition technology in the private sector as well as for the police.

### The reframe

The convenience is real and Revolut's examples of it are ordinary: hands full, card at the bottom of a bag. Nobody is forced to use the option, and it is offered to Revolut's own customers who have already given their biometrics at sign-up. The company's statement that the data is encrypted and not kept by merchants is a statement about its design, and campaigners and a university academic have answered it with statements about risk. This entry does not decide between them.

The manuscript is concerned with a different part of the story. It says that privacy once depended on friction. Information existed, but finding it, connecting it and interpreting it were all difficult, and that difficulty was a protection that nobody had to arrange. Modern systems reverse the relationship. Individual facts remain ordinary and become revealing in combination.

A face is the plainest example. It is shown to the street all day. Nobody has ever thought of it as a secret. What the terminal does is turn it into a key, a token that proves who you are to a bank, and a token has a property that a face does not usually have, which is that it is checked against a record. The professor's point about the password is about that property. A password that leaks can be changed. The thing it was replaced by cannot.

The second phrase in her comment is the one the manuscript's argument turns on: compliance becomes passive. At a till that asks for a card, the customer does something, and at one that asks for a face, the customer is simply there. The chapter describes the stage at which systems stop responding to what a person does and begin to respond to what the person is. It asks how much inference can occur before people stop feeling understood and begin feeling categorised. A coffee at a Bloomsbury cafe is a modest place to ask it, and the answer will not come from one café. It will come from the third and the thirtieth, each of them reasonable on its own.
