### The story

Andy Burnham will put plans for a free-to-use National Care Service, based on the NHS, to voters at the next election. People would no longer have to sell their home to fund care in old age or illness, as "everyone contributes, everyone is covered", he said. The think-tank the Health Foundation estimates it will cost at least £18.5 billion a year, with tax rises almost certainly paying for it. Challenged over the cost, Mr Burnham told the BBC: "People are paying care charges now, spending tens of thousands of savings, sometimes losing homes." He said social care in England is "as unfair as US healthcare", with the most vulnerable able to lose everything. People with more than £23,250 get no help with care costs from their council in England. Shadow home secretary Chris Philp said the funds must come from cutting welfare spending but that Mr Burnham would "reach for tax increases that will simply cause even more damage to our economy". Mr Burnham said he would not leave office without grasping the nettle, "recognising it might be difficult or unpopular". The plan would go into his manifesto because it needs public approval.

### The reframe

This is a proposal, not a law, and its price is a fair thing to argue about. The Health Foundation puts the cost at least £18.5 billion a year, and Mr Philp says the money should come from welfare. Mr Burnham says there is already a cost, and that it falls on individuals. Each of these can be checked against figures when they are published.

The manuscript is interested in one narrow feature of the present system: the savings threshold. A person with more than £23,250 gets no council help, so the test of need is a test of what they own. Before help arrives, assets have to be shown and, in Mr Burnham's account, spent. The manuscript describes safety nets as places where access is conditional and need is treated with suspicion, and where claimants must demonstrate repeatedly that they qualify. A universal service would remove that step. It would replace proof of poverty with membership, which is how the NHS already works.

That is a description of a mechanism, not a case for the plan. A universal service has its own conditions, and they sit in the tax system and the budget. The manuscript's concern is where a society places its scrutiny. Under the present arrangement it sits on the person needing care and their savings. Under the proposed one it would sit on the general account, and the argument Mr Philp and the Health Foundation are having is about whether that account can carry it.
