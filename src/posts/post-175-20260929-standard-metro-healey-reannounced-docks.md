### The story

Chancellor John Healey told the Labour conference that the money New Labour poured in during the 1990s is "simply not there now". He said: "The cost of the nation's debt is not just a number in a budget. It's money we can't spend on the NHS, schools, housing, social care, policing our streets, controlling our borders or defending our country." Hinting at tax rises, he said debt levels are "an assault on our common sense" and that he and the Prime Minister are in lockstep that the fiscal rules will be met. He announced an apprenticeship scheme overseen by local mayors, saying "I refuse to write off a million young people like the Tories did", and promised "a new age of industrialisation" thanks to cash for a new Royal Navy submarine base at Faslane, which Metro reports is part of a £15 billion upgrade announced previously. Shadow chancellor Andrew Griffith said Mr Healey, who resigned warning that Labour's failure to fund defence was making Britain unsafe, still would not fund the 3% target he walked out over. "All they offer is reannounced docks and hot air," he said.

### The reframe

Mr Healey was open about the constraint. He told delegates the money is not there, and the sentence about the cost of the nation's debt was a plain one. This entry takes that at face value.

The report also contains a fact that does not depend on whose side anyone is on. The Faslane money is described as part of a £15 billion upgrade announced previously. Mr Griffith's word for that is "reannounced", and it is an opposition word, but the report supplies the underlying fact. A figure that was announced earlier has been presented again in a speech about a new age.

That is normal. Every party reuses its own earlier commitments in a new setting, and a conference is a place for it. What the manuscript notes is the effect on the listener. An announcement is immediate and visible and gains credit when it is made, and credit is not given again when the work is completed, so there is an incentive to announce the same thing more than once. The manuscript says that political systems reward visibility more reliably than delivery.

The reader of the report cannot tell, from the speech, how much of the "new age" is new money. That is the useful question to take away. Not whether the base is a good idea, which the speech does not argue, but how much of the funding was already counted in the sums the chancellor says are not there. The Budget statement is where an answer, if there is one, would be set out.
