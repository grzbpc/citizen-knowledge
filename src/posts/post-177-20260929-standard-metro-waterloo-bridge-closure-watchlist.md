### The story

A report has revealed structural issues with Waterloo Bridge, leading to fears it may have to close. Waterloo is one of the busiest bridges in the capital and an inspection revealed that high volumes of traffic have taken their toll. Issues include water damage and cracks up to 2mm wide, Westminster Council documents say. The report says there could be a "full closure of Waterloo Bridge" unless weight limits are introduced or repairs are carried out by the end of the 2029-30 financial year. A council spokesperson said: "Waterloo Bridge remains safe and we have no immediate concerns about its structural integrity." The council said it is taking a "proactive approach to its long-term maintenance and resilience". Structural issues have already closed the Hammersmith and Albert bridges to vehicle traffic. Westminster, Vauxhall and Lambeth bridges were placed on a "critical" watchlist by Transport for London after they scored under 40 in inspections, a benchmark prompting action or investigation.

### The reframe

The council says the bridge is safe and the report gives no evidence to the contrary. This entry takes that at face value. What interests the manuscript is how the situation is being handled.

The bridge is described through instruments: an inspection, a score, a benchmark of 40, a watchlist, a weight limit and a deadline. The deadline is the end of the 2029-30 financial year, more than three years away. A score of under 40 prompts "action or investigation", which means the response the threshold requires can be an investigation. Each of these is a sensible way to manage risk in an old structure, and none of them is a repair.

The manuscript distinguishes a crisis from a condition. A crisis demands resolution, and a condition invites containment. The distinction is made there about homelessness, and this entry does not claim the cases are alike. It borrows the idea for the way a long run of problems can be handled. Hammersmith and Albert bridges are closed to vehicles, three more are on the critical list, and Waterloo now has a date on which it might close. Taken one at a time, each is a managed risk. Taken together, they describe a set of structures whose condition is known and whose resolution is scheduled for later.

The report does not say what a repair would cost or who would pay, and that is the figure a reader would need. Until it is given, the sentence "we have no immediate concerns" and the sentence "a full closure" are both true at once.
