### The story

Manchester City have been found to have breached the Premier League's financial rules, with an independent commission upholding 114 of the 115 charges. The League said the commission found "sham" arrangements that inflated the club's revenue and reduced its costs by more than £900 million between 2009 and 2018. Falsified deals with a number of sponsors were part of a disguised funding scheme, the League said, in which those companies were only required to pay part of the relevant sponsorship fees, with the rest funded by Abu Dhabi United Group Investment and Development, which owned the club. The commission found that the club had filed misstated accounts and concealed the true state of its finances from auditors and regulators, and concluded that "by its conduct the club clearly intended to circumvent the Premier League rules". One charge, relating to an alleged failure to co-operate with the investigation, was not proved. The sanction will be addressed at a separate hearing.

Premier League chief executive Richard Masters called the decision "the most significant in Premier League history" and said the League would move "swiftly through the remainder of the process, to provide certainty for the League, our clubs and fans". The commission said it was "regrettable" that a decision had taken so long; the investigation began in 2018. City said they were "disappointed and surprised" and will appeal "on the basis that the opinion contains clear material errors, of law, principle and fact, and is unsafe". The ultimate sanction available is expulsion, which would need the support of at least 15 of the 20 clubs. Javier Tebas, president of Spain's LaLiga, said the sanction should be "intended to set an example".

### The reframe, with the manuscript

The findings are those of a commission, the club disputes them and has said it will appeal, and nothing about the sanction has been decided. This entry does not take a view on the case. It takes the commission's account as an account, and asks what kind of problem it describes.

The charges concern financial rules, which depend on reporting. A club reports its revenue and costs, and auditors and regulators check the reports. The commission's finding is that the reports were misstated and that the true state of the club's finances was concealed from the people who check them. If that is upheld, the rules were in place and the paperwork was in order, and the substance did not match. The manuscript's section on the cost of appearance opens with the general form of this.

> *When rules lose their ability to guide behaviour, they do not vanish. They shift function.*

The section is about rules that survive through display, and it is careful to say that the behaviour is not necessarily deceit. The commission's finding goes further than that, since it says the club "clearly intended to circumvent" the rules. The manuscript's point is the setting in which such a thing is possible: a system that relies on the appearance of compliance can only be as good as the checking behind the appearance.

The manuscript also says what such a system depends on.

> *Order is maintained, but only at the surface.*

The report gives one measure of how long the surface held. The conduct covers 2009 to 2018 and the investigation began in 2018, so the verdict arrives eight years after the inquiry opened. The commission itself called the delay regrettable. A long case about sponsorship deals and accounts may be unavoidably slow, and Mr Masters says the League is committed to moving swiftly now. The club says it will challenge the commission's reasoning on appeal, on points of law and fact. Both are part of how a legitimate process works.

What remains is the sanction. Mr Tebas has said it should be made to set an example. Whether it is decided in terms of the rules and the findings, or in terms of the example, is the thing to look for when the next hearing reports.
