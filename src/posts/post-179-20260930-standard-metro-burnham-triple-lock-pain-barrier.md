### The story

Andy Burnham told the Labour conference that he will bring water companies back under state control, drop VAT on electricity bills and create a National Care Service that is free at the point of entry, which he called a landmark policy as "important as the creation of the NHS itself". Paying for it will require changes to the state pension, he announced. The triple lock will end in 2030, and the pension will then rise only in line with inflation or by 2.5%, whichever is higher, with the link to wage growth removed. "I accept I may pay a political price," he said. "But someone has to go through the pain barrier and rip the plaster off." The Institute for Fiscal Studies said ditching one element of the triple lock was "an improvement but not perfect", and warned that this alone will not raise enough to fund universal social care. Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch said his social care plans were "falling apart under scrutiny" and that "more tax rises are inevitable". Liberal Democrat leader Ed Davey said it was "wrong" to scrap the triple lock, which he said had been "essential in the fight against pensioner poverty". Reform UK's Nigel Farage said "Andy Burnham will cut your pension". Metro's political columnist called it "a genuine, deliberate political risk".

### The reframe

The manuscript's chapter on symbolic politics ends on a point about correction. A system can survive for a long time on reassurance, it says, but correcting itself requires acknowledgement, and acknowledgement requires honesty about what the reassurance has been doing.

By that standard, the Prime Minister's sentence about the political price is notable. He named a cost, said who would carry it and said he expected to be criticised for it. Naming a cost in advance is not the pattern the manuscript describes, in which commitments are expressed without first resolving the conditions that made them necessary. It does not follow that the plan will work, and this entry takes no view on the policy.

The report also contains the other half of the manuscript's test. The Institute for Fiscal Studies says the pension change alone will not raise enough to fund the care service. The Health Foundation, in the report Metro ran on Monday, put the cost at least £18.5 billion a year. If both are right, the gap between the two is the part of the plan not yet explained, and it is the first thing the critics have gone to. Mr Badenoch's phrase for it is that more tax rises are inevitable, and Mr Davey and Mr Farage have attacked the pension element from different directions. The report gives a date for the saving and none for the service it is meant to pay for.

One other fact in the report is relevant. The change takes effect in 2030, and Metro reported on Monday that the next election is likely in 2029. If that holds, voters will have decided before the change takes effect. Put in a manifesto, the cost would at least be tested by voters before it is incurred.
