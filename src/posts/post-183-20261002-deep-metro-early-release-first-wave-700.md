### The story

About 700 prisoners walked free from jails across England on Thursday, the first wave of 4,500 who will be released over the next year. Under the government scheme, offenders jailed for fraud, domestic abuse and coercive control are now eligible for release after serving a third of their sentence. Metro reported inmates cheering outside Durham, Leeds, Brixton and Bristol. One man said he would "be back inside in a month or two", adding: "I get out, take beer, drugs... Go back in, put a bit of weight on, do it all again." Another man leaving HMP Wandsworth was arrested by British Transport Police outside, on claims that he stole two bikes from stations during the summer.

Downing Street's insistence that prisons are on the brink of collapse is "100% right", justice secretary Alex Norris said. "There's not a cell free," he added. No.10 said released offenders remain under strict monitoring, including greater use of electronic tags, and victims minister Alex Davies-Jones said £500 million will be spent supporting victims. The Conservatives say the scheme puts the public at risk and is an "insult to victims". Domestic abuse survivors told Metro they are living in "sheer terror". One woman, whose ex-partner has been freed five months into an 18-month term, said she is worried he will come back. Another asked: "How do the government have the right to overrule a judge?" Mr Norris said he understood victims' anxiety but that "the greatest danger would be prisons running out of cells for criminals, which was the crisis we inherited". The government plans to build 14,000 prison places. After a backlash this summer, the prime minister announced that people convicted of manslaughter and sex offences, along with two men in a car when PC Andrew Harper was killed in 2019, would not be freed early.

### The reframe, with the manuscript

This entry does not argue for or against the scheme. The justice secretary says the alternative is a prison system that has run out of room, and that is a serious claim which Metro does not test. The people who spoke to the paper say that a sentence passed by a judge is not being served. Both of those things can be true at the same time, and the interest here is in what the arrangement says about the cost of running a system of punishment.

A sentence has two lengths. There is the one the judge passes, and there is the one that is served, and the gap between them is decided by a cell count. The manuscript's chapter on discretion ends with a short passage on what happens when a system asks for compliance while its costs grow. It does not say that compliance stops. It says what happens to the belief that used to go with it.

> *Participation continues, but belief thins.*

The report gives two kinds of cost. One is the money, £500 million for victims and 14,000 places still to be built. The other is harder to put a figure on, and it is the confidence of the people who were told that the sentence meant something. It is worth noticing that the minister does not say the release is a good thing in itself. He says it is better than the alternative, which is a different kind of statement.

The passage names the condition under which that happens.

> *When enforcement consumes disproportionate resources without producing proportionate benefit, legitimacy weakens.*

Whether that describes the present case is a question for the figures, and the report does not supply them. What it does show is a man who says he will be back inside within weeks, and another stopped at the gate on an allegation he has not answered. They are two stories out of 700, and they cannot be read as a measure of the scheme. The measure would be how many of those released are back in custody a year from now, and that figure will not exist until next autumn. Until then the argument will be conducted between a minister's cell count and a victim's fear, and neither can yet be checked.
