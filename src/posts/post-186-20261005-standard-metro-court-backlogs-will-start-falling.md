### The story

Delays in the justice system will decrease in this parliament despite the scrapping of the plan to curb jury trials, the justice secretary has said. Alex Norris admitted that bringing in legislation for judge-only trials, as suggested by former prime minister Keir Starmer, would be "complicated to implement". He insisted the backlogs will still drop without cutting jury trials, but did not say by how much. "They will start falling in this parliament, and over the next decade that backlog falls significantly as a result of the changes we're making," he told BBC1's Sunday With Laura Kuenssberg. The Criminal Bar Association has warned that the proposals will shift 25,000 cases a year, including 5,500 jury trials, on to magistrates, who it said are "completely unequipped" to try complex assaults, sexual assaults and child abuse. Shadow justice secretary Nick Timothy said Labour's move was "restricting juries by the back door".

### The reframe

Two things are being argued about at once, and it helps to keep them apart. One is whether jury trials should be reduced, which is a question of principle on which the bar and the government disagree. The other is how long people wait for their case to be heard. The minister's answer concerns the second, and the report records his words closely enough to see their shape.

The backlog "will start falling", and over a decade it "falls significantly". Neither phrase is attached to a number. A falling backlog is an improvement on a rising one, so the forecast has real content, but without a size or a date it cannot yet be checked by anyone who is waiting.

The manuscript's chapter on managing failure is about homelessness, but its central observation is more general. It describes a condition that is administered: counted, reported, discussed and held at a steady level, while the thing that would end it stays outside the process. It speaks of suspended resolution. A court backlog is a different problem, and this is not a claim that the two are alike. The point is a narrower one about language. A promise that a queue will shorten tells the people in it that someone is working on the problem. It does not tell them when their own case will be heard, and for a witness or a defendant that is the only part that matters.
