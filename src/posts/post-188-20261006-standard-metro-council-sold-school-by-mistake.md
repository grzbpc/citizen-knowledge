### The story

A businessman who bought a £395,000 house from Birmingham council at auction in 2013 has learned that he also appears to own a nearby school. Twelve years later, Sukhjit Bains was told that he had been handed ownership of George Dixon primary school. The error happened, according to the report, when his solicitor accidentally sent the wrong paperwork and the council's conveyancer mistakenly rubberstamped the deal. The school and the house are registered under the same land registry number. Only one form, not the two needed, was completed, so the house and the land the school sits on were transferred together. The mistake was only uncovered in 2024, when the school considered a bid to become an academy and learned that the council did not own the building.

The council, which went bankrupt in 2023, says Mr Bains initially agreed to hand back control and then stopped replying to its letters and declined to sign papers. It has launched a High Court action against him and the lender, Lloyds Bank, to win back ownership of the 324-pupil school. It says attempts to sell the school or playing fields cannot go ahead without the consent of the secretary of state. Mr Bains declined to comment to a reporter. His lawyer said the 2013 transfer was to his company and not to him as an individual.

### The reframe

The facts are in dispute and the court will decide them. The council's account is that the sale was a mistake, the buyer's side says the transfer was to a company, and nothing in the report says that anyone acted dishonestly. The useful part is what the account shows about scale.

The sale of a school depends on rules, and the report mentions one of them: consent from the secretary of state. That rule is not a small one. In this account it was never triggered, because the transfer did not come to anyone's attention as the sale of a school. It came to the council as a house, with one form where two were needed. The manuscript calls this kind of event a small variance with a large consequence. A form not completed, or completed once instead of twice, decides who owns a building with 324 pupils in it, and the difference went unnoticed for eleven years.

The manuscript's point is about people, not buildings. Outcomes hang on moments that nobody saw as important when they happened, and the explanations that follow tend to sound reasonable because the alternative can no longer be seen. Here the alternative can be seen, because the council can name the missing form. A reader may notice how unusual that is. Most such variances never surface, and a school had to consider becoming an academy before this one did.
