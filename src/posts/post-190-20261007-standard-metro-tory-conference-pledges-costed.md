### The story

The Conservatives have pledged to halve employer national insurance on jobs worked by young people if they win the next election. The flat rate would be cut from 15% to 7.5% for workers aged 21 to 24, at an estimated cost of £2.3 billion. Leader Kemi Badenoch insisted the plan was "fiscally credible", and Tory officials said a funding plan would be published on Wednesday. Asked why the rate would be left the same for most employees, she told the BBC: "If I could afford to cut it for everybody, I promise you that I would. But young people have a much worse problem than everybody else."

The party also set out plans for a new "Britannia Shield" missile defence system for the UK, designed to bring down drones and missiles. Officials called it "in essence, a British 'iron dome'", though Mrs Badenoch said she was not replicating Israel's system. It would cost up to £10 billion, which shadow defence secretary James Cartlidge said was "12 times more than Labour" announced for similar capabilities in their defence plan. The system would come with "parallel" protections for overseas territories.

### The reframe

Both pledges are made by a party that is not in government, in the week of its conference, and both come with a price. That is more than many announcements offer. The cost is stated, and for the first pledge a funding plan was promised the next day. A reader can check a figure, and that is a fair test of a policy.

The manuscript's argument is about a different part of the process. It observes that politics tends to reward the announcement at the moment it is made, while the delivery comes years later, if at all. The reward does not depend on whether the thing is built. That is true of every party, and it is true of the government's own conference promises of the week before.

What is not in the report is the second half of each pledge. For the tax cut, the report does not say how the £2.3 billion would be found, only that a plan would follow. For the shield, there is a cost ceiling and no timetable. Both depend on winning an election that has not been called. The pledges are real as statements of intent. They become policies when a government with a budget decides to spend the money, and the difference is the thing to remember when the next set of conference announcements arrives.
