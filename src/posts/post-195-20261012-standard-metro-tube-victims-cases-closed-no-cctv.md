### The story

Victims of crime on public transport in London are being "failed by fundamental flaws" in the reporting process and a lack of CCTV, according to London TravelWatch. Confusion about how to report incidents, and a lack of confidence that anything will happen, is preventing people from coming forward. TravelWatch found that only two out of five victims come forward, and called for a single system to simplify reporting. Victims also told Metro their cases were dropped because basic forms of evidence, including CCTV, were not working.

One woman said that in 2021 a man on the Elizabeth Line threatened to rape and kill her and slapped her after she refused to give him her number. She reported it to British Transport Police, but officers said they could not pursue the case because the CCTV was not working. She said she could not use the line for months afterwards. Another woman said staff were uninterested after she reported being upskirted on the Central Line in March; a member of staff told her to text British Transport Police, and the police later closed the case because there was no CCTV in the carriage. BTP chief inspector Cheryl Ling agreed that "reporting pathways should be straightforward". Siwan Hayward, TfL's director of security, policing and enforcement, apologised to both women and said TfL is working closely with the police "to strengthen reporting, support investigations and bring offenders to justice".

### The reframe

The law is not in doubt here. Threats and upskirting are offences. What the report describes is a gap between the existence of the offence and the ability to act on it, and the gap is made of ordinary things: a camera that does not record, a member of staff who does not know the route for a report, a form that is not the same on every line.

The manuscript's chapter on the illusion of law is concerned with this gap. It describes a system that can maintain the appearance of control in the abstract while the practical means of enforcing it are thin, and it observes that people learn this quickly. The figure from TravelWatch is the clearest sign of that learning. If three out of five victims do not report, the missing reports are not recorded as failures of enforcement. They are simply not counted, and the picture that results looks calmer than it is.

The responses on record are apologies and a commitment to work with the police, which is what an organisation can say at this stage. Neither the report nor the statements give a date by which cameras will work in every carriage or by which there will be a single route for reporting. Those are the details that would show whether the fault is being repaired.
