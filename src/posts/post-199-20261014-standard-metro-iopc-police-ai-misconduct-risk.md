### The story

Police could accidentally commit misconduct by using AI to help solve cases, the Independent Office for Police Conduct (IOPC) has said. It told police chiefs to review their policies so that staff know the boundaries for the technology. "There's a risk officers and staff, believing they're doing the right thing by using AI, perhaps to speed up a process, could end up being investigated for misconduct because they don't know or understand what's allowed," said director general Rachel Watson.

### The reframe

This is a sensible warning, and it comes from the body that would carry out such an investigation. It asks chiefs to do something specific, which is to write down what is allowed. The report does not say how many officers use AI tools now or whether any has been investigated.

The manuscript has a chapter on what happens when people do not know where the line is. It describes a situation in which the question changes from "Am I within the rule?" to "Am I close enough to attract attention?", and in which the person who is penalised often finds out where the line was only afterwards. That is a condition of the rule not being published in a form the person can use, and it is not a fault in the person. An officer who uses a tool to save time on a case, in good faith, is in the position the manuscript describes: acting reasonably in a space where the limit has not been drawn.

There is also a point about order. The IOPC is warning about the risk before a case has arisen, and that is the better time to do it. The same arrangement in many other settings is discovered afterwards, by the person who has been told the rule was always there. The test of this warning is whether the forces publish their policies, so that an officer can read the boundary before reaching it.
