### The story

Around one in six working-age people receiving disability benefits face no extra costs due to their condition, a study has found. Personal independence payment, known as PIP, is designed to help with the additional costs that can come with being disabled. The Resolution Foundation said its research showed that 16% of claimants reported no costs due to their condition. The survey of 2,611 PIP recipients aged 16 to 64 in England and Wales found that 12% of those with no costs had low limitations, while 4% had no costs but higher-level limitations. The think tank estimated £4.4 billion could be saved if payments were stopped or lowered, and said such a change would affect younger claimants in particular, after finding that a fifth (21%) of 16 to 24-year-olds reported little limitation and no additional costs. The Foundation also suggested one-off payments, rather than ongoing ones, for people who reported a particular cost, such as a mobility aid. Louise Murphy, of the Resolution Foundation, said of the system: "Recipients don't trust it, awards don't fully reflect the nature of disability today and spending is rising unsustainably."

### The reframe

This is a study by a think tank, and it should be read as one. The question of what a disability payment is for, and who should receive it, is a legitimate one for public debate, and the figure of £4.4 billion is a real sum. Nothing here is a case against asking.

The manuscript's chapter on the safety net describes a particular habit in how support is measured. A benefit is judged by what it costs and by whether the person receiving it appears to meet the test, and the account that the claimant gives of their own situation becomes the evidence. In this survey the evidence is the answer to a question about costs. A person who reports none is counted in the 16%, and a person whose costs are hard to put into a survey answer may be counted there too.

The detail the report gives is balanced. Of the 16%, three quarters had low limitations and one quarter had higher ones, and the person whose figures say they have no costs may still have a serious condition. The Foundation itself says recipients do not trust the system, which is a finding about the process as well as the money. The test of any reform will be whether it is built on that second finding as well as the first.
