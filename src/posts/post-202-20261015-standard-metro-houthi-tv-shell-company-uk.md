### The story

A Houthi-controlled television station has had a base in the UK for the last 12 years, Metro can reveal. Al-Masirah TV, run by the Iran-backed militants, has operated a "shell company" in London since 2014, according to Companies House documents. The channel is the main propaganda network for the Yemen-based Houthi rebel group, which attacked King Khalid International Airport in Saudi Arabia last week, killing 12 and injuring 300. Al-Masirah, headquartered in Beirut, was blocked by the US government in 2021 after being accused of spreading pro-Iran disinformation.

The filing for Almasirah Network Limited shows a "television programming and broadcasting activities" business, first registered in September 2014. One director is listed as Ammar Al-Hamzy, an alternative spelling of the name of the current director general of Al-Masirah TV. Another is listed as Embrahim Mohammed Al-Dailami, an alternative spelling of the name of the Houthi ambassador to Iran. Financial accountant Ben Crowne told Metro the company is "clearly a shell company", with no material assets (£200), ten years of dormant accounts, and recent filings overdue. US intelligence expert Jonathan Hackett said Iran uses dormant media outlets "to keep potential access points on ice". Conservative MP Iain Duncan Smith called the links "shocking" and former home secretary Priti Patel said any potential Houthi operations in Britain "must be fully investigated". Al-Masirah was not contactable for comment.

### The reframe

This entry makes no claim about what the company was used for. The report says it has assets of £200 and has filed dormant accounts, and the experts quoted draw their own conclusions. The part that can be stated with confidence is how the information was held. The filings were public, they were on the register for twelve years, and the names of the directors were there to be read.

The manuscript's chapter on surveillance makes a point about how information stays out of view. For most of modern history, it says, privacy relied less on secrecy than on friction: the information existed, but it was hard to put together. A company register is a good example. Each entry is accessible, and it takes a person who knows the right names and has a reason to ask to connect an entry to a wider picture. Here the connection was made by a journalist and an accountant.

That friction works in both directions. It protects ordinary people whose records are scattered. It also means that something which should be noticed can sit in plain sight for years. The report does not say who, if anyone, was responsible for checking, and that is the question it leaves open.
