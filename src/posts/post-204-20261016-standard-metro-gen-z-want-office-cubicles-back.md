### The story

Gen Z workers are asking bosses to reintroduce a 1990s office staple, the humble cubicle, to save their sanity and stop them feeling "overstimulated". "I wish I had office cubicles like my parents did," one wrote on Reddit. The 18 to 29-year-olds entered the workforce in the age of the open-plan office, where privacy is at a premium. They find such a set-up "hella overstimulating", many said, adding that "no sound dampening makes phone conversations harder". Nearly a quarter (24%) have taken time off due to noise-related stress, a poll by office architecture provider Oscar Acoustics found.

Leadership coach Harriet Minter told Metro: "Gen Z are asking to be able to concentrate, which is what their parents had by default. Open plan asks everyone to agree on rules nobody ever wrote down. Every one of those becomes a small negotiation, and people are exhausted by negotiating." Psychotherapist Amanda Macdonald said a cubicle offers a halfway point: "This sense of having a space that isn't home but still yours gives people a sense of belonging."

### The reframe

The poll comes from a company that sells office acoustics, so the figure should be read with that in mind, and the report does not say how many people were asked. The observation from Ms Minter is the useful part, because it can be understood without the poll.

An open-plan office has a floor plan and, usually, a policy. It does not have a written answer to how loud a phone call may be, whether a colleague can be interrupted, or when headphones mean "do not disturb". These are settled by each person watching what others do and by what happens when someone gets it wrong. The manuscript calls this the quiet curriculum. It argues that people learn how institutions work not from what they are told but from small inconsistencies and the reactions that follow, and that the official language of instruction can stay in place while the practical understanding takes over.

The workers in the report are describing the cost of that arrangement. A rule that is written can be read once. A rule that is negotiated has to be re-learned each time the room changes, and people who are new to a workplace are the ones who have the least information. The cubicle, on this account, is less a nostalgia than a way of removing a daily set of questions that no one had agreed to ask.
