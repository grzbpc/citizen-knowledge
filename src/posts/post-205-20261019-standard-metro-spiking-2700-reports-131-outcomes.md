### The story

Spikers say they target people's drinks "for a laugh" or to "liven up the party", according to a study by the University of Birmingham and the Home Office. Some even justified their actions as "generous" because they "helped people enjoy their nights". Drug-laced vapes and tablets such as antihistamines or sleep aids are used, the researchers found, as well as cannabis stewed in tea and, in one case, a high-sugar soft drink put in a diabetic person's alcoholic drink. According to the Met, the most common form is through alcohol. The results were released as the minister for safeguarding, Satvir Kaur, launched an anti-spiking campaign that shifts the focus on to perpetrators. "We want people to feel confident to come forward to the police," she said, adding that the campaign comes as young people head to university and reports jump. A new law means spiking now carries a prison sentence of up to ten years.

In the year ending in September, more than 2,700 spiking-related offences were reported to the Metropolitan Police. Only 131 led to a charge, caution or summons. Det Supt Dan Thompson, the Met's spiking lead, told victims that whatever they have been spiked with "can flush out of your system quickly" and that it is important to test straight away "so we can get the evidence while it is there". A victim who was spiked in 2020 told Metro that paramedics told her she was drunk and that her friends had to advocate for her.

### The reframe

Both the law and the campaign are real steps. A maximum sentence of ten years says the offence is serious, and a campaign that addresses those who spike, not those who are spiked, changes whom the message is aimed at. The study of why people do it is useful in the same way.

The manuscript's chapter on discretion describes a second function of enforcement alongside the practical one, which is to be seen to be acting. It does not say that the visible part is insincere. It says that the visible part and the practical part can run at different speeds, and that the public hears the first one more clearly.

The Met's figures give the practical part. Of more than 2,700 reports, 131 resulted in a formal outcome, which is below one in twenty. The report does not say why, though the detective's advice offers one clue: the evidence leaves the body quickly, and a victim who does not know they have been spiked cannot test in time. A victim in the report was told she was drunk. That is where the practical part of enforcement is decided, long before any sentence is set. A rise in the maximum penalty does not reach that point, and the next set of figures will show whether anything else has.
