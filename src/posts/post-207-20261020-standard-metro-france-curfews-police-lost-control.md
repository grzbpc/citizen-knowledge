### The story

French towns and cities have been told to declare curfews in a bid to end days of rioting and looting, after ministers admitted police "lost control". Despite weeks of rising violence across the country, sparked by student protests, interior minister Laurent Nuñez said there had been a "lack of anticipation" from officers. His offer to quit was rejected by president Emmanuel Macron, whose appeal for national unity in a live television address seemed only to further enrage protesters, Metro reports. Mr Macron vowed that "all necessary lessons will be drawn from intelligence failures". Prime minister Sébastien Lecornu said it was "a miracle no one was killed" and that he is "fully resolved to restoring public order". He gave regional leaders powers to declare "partial and very localised" curfews from last night, and warned that the government will declare a state of emergency if necessary. "We will not be dictated and ruled by the breakers, by the thieves, by the thugs," he said, adding that intelligence agencies had told the government of "an extreme-left movement looking to create disorder" up to next year's presidential election. More than half a million people took to the streets nationwide.

### The reframe

This entry takes no view on the protests or on the government's description of those behind them, which is the government's account. It looks at the sentences the ministers chose.

There are two kinds in the report. One kind is an admission: police "lost control", there was a "lack of anticipation", there were "intelligence failures". These are plain statements, and they should be credited. The other kind is a promise: lessons "will be drawn", order will be restored, a state of emergency will be declared "if necessary". The promise describes what is intended, and the report gives no detail of what the lessons will be or how anyone outside the government could tell that they had been learned.

The manuscript's chapter on symbolic politics describes how language fills the space between a problem and its resolution. It says this does not need dishonesty. It arises because a government under pressure must say something at once, and the thing that can be said at once is a statement of intent. The sensible way to read the French statements is to hold the two kinds apart. The admissions are information about the past. The promises are a description of the future, and later reporting will show whether the curfews, which are limited and local by design, are followed by anything that can be measured.
