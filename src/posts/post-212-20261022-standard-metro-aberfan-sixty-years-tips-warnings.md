### The story

The King told the people of Aberfan they will "always" be in his family's hearts, as he marked the 60th anniversary of the disaster which claimed the lives of 116 children and 28 adults. On 21 October 1966, 150,000 tonnes of coal waste slid down a hillside after days of heavy rain and engulfed Pantglas Junior School, on the last day before half-term. The King, Queen and Prince of Wales joined survivors and bereaved families in the village, visiting the memorial garden on the site of the school, where a prayer and a moment of silence were held.

Survivors told Metro of the day and of what followed. For years, locals and the school's headteacher had warned about the No 7 tip, which loomed over the village and was built over two underground streams. A tribunal blamed the National Coal Board, but nobody was prosecuted and bereaved families received just £500 in compensation. Later the government took £150,000 from a charity fund to remove the tips, Metro reports, and the money was returned in 1997. Gaynor Madgwick, who was swept away by the slurry and lost her brother and sister, met prime minister Andy Burnham in Aberfan on Monday. "It's taken 60 years, and it'll probably take many more... to actually have some peace from it," she said. Both she and another survivor, David Davies, have spent decades campaigning for justice.

### The reframe

The facts of the disaster and of the tribunal are settled, and this entry does not add to them. It follows only what the Metro report sets side by side: a warning that had been given for years, a finding that the National Coal Board was to blame, no prosecution, and £500 for each bereaved family.

The manuscript has a chapter on how responsibility and consequence relate. Its argument is that, in practice, consequences run according to exposure and not according to the size of what was done. People in a position to be held to account tend to be held to it lightly, and people with no means of protecting themselves bear the cost. It is a general claim, and Aberfan is one of its hardest tests because the numbers are so plain: a tribunal's blame on one side, and on the other a sum of £500 paid to families.

The survivors do not speak in the language of a theory. They speak of waiting, of campaigning and of what a community carried. The King's words are about the present and about the people. The report records the other side as well, the public money taken from the charity fund, and the 1997 return. Sixty years on, those are the details survivors still bring up, and they suggest that the question of who bears a loss is not closed by a ceremony, however sincere.
