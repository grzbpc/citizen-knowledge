### The story

Police have raided 400 shops, market stalls and car boot sales and seized more than £100,000 worth of property in what the Met describes as its biggest ever crackdown on the resale of stolen goods. Closure orders were issued to 11 shops and 42 people were arrested as part of Operation Zoridon. Police allege that some rogue traders bought stolen items from shoplifters or thieves who have been taking smartphones from across the capital, and that many items are stolen to order from major high street retailers.

In one shop in Hounslow, west London, police seized 300 phones and £15,000 in cash. In another raid there, five pallets of Dyson products were taken and three people arrested. Drugs, stolen passports and a machete were also found, which the Met says is proof that the resale of stolen goods fuels organised crime. Metro was invited to join a raid on a phone shop in Brixton, where three workers were taken into custody on suspicion of handling stolen goods and officers checked stock with UV torches, because some devices carry a chemical coating that lets police trace who they are registered to. Several devices were believed to be stolen, and one had been snatched years earlier. Items that turn out not to be stolen will be returned when the investigation ends, an officer told a customer who had left a phone for repair.

Deputy Commissioner Matt Jukes said the operation was launched because some of these shops undercut legitimate businesses and because the public is concerned. "If you profit from retail crime, you will be arrested," he said. Warning notices have also been issued to car boot sale organisers. Metro adds that shoplifting fell by 3.7 per cent, or 3,500 offences, in London last year.

### The reframe

The facts of the operation come from the Met and from what Metro saw on one raid, and this entry takes them as reported. The raids did what the force said they would do, and the people whose phones were held for checking were told they would get them back if they were not stolen. A resale market for stolen goods is a real part of why theft pays, and going after it is a reasonable thing for police to do.

What the report also shows is how enforcement of this kind is described. The manuscript argues that public debate about enforcement rarely begins with cost, and that it begins instead with deterrence, order and safety. Here the numbers offered are the ones that count activity: 400 premises, 11 closure orders, 42 arrests, £100,000 of property. They are real figures, and they are also the kind that fit a single headline. The last line of the article gives a different kind of number, a fall in shoplifting of 3.7 per cent, and the report does not say how that fall relates to the operation. It cannot, since it covers the year before.

The question the manuscript raises is a modest one. A visible operation shows effort at every stage of the chain, from the shoplifter to the shop. What it does not show by itself is what the effort costs or what changes afterwards. A crowd outside a Brixton shop waiting for phones that were not stolen is a small reminder that enforcement is carried out on people as well as on a market, and that the way it is counted is a choice.
