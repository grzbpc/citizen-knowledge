### The story

Everyone sleeping rough in England will be offered a safe route away from the streets this winter, Andy Burnham has promised. The prime minister made the comments before the chancellor, John Healey, confirms a near-doubling of financial help for rough sleepers in his Budget on Wednesday. A further £432 million will be provided on top of £442 million committed earlier this year to offer winter accommodation before Christmas. Mr Burnham is hosting a summit on rough sleeping at Downing Street today, with attendees including the National Housing Federation, Virgin Media O2, Ikea and Comic Relief.

"Rough sleeping isn't a fact of life, it's a thing governments choose to accept or don't," the prime minister said. "Well, I don't accept it." He said the funding would continue for the rest of this parliament, but "we can't do it alone". The latest figures from the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government estimate that 4,793 people were sleeping rough on a single night in England last autumn. Housing secretary Angela Rayner called it "a big ambition". Shadow housing secretary Katie Lam said rough sleeping has hit record highs under Labour and that the answer is "always more taxpayers' money and more empty promises".

Metro also spoke to Gary Clarke, 66, a St Mungo's volunteer who patrols Reading town centre early in the morning to find people sleeping rough so outreach teams can help them. He sees between 20 and 30 people, some of whom have lived in tents for years. Some, he says, do not accept housing offers and "just want to be left alone". When the temperature falls below zero, the council funds additional emergency accommodation, and he said he despairs of political dialogue suggesting there is an easy solution.

### The reframe

Both sides in the report offer numbers and neither can settle the matter from the page. The government gives funding figures and a promise of an offer for everyone. The Conservatives give a claim about records and an objection to spending. The count of 4,793 is an estimate for one night, and the winter offer is described as a route off the streets, which is different from a home. This entry takes no position on which party is right.

The manuscript's argument is narrower. It says that homelessness is described as a crisis but treated in practice as a condition to be managed, with responses that concentrate on visibility and keep the problem within acceptable limits, and that outreach produces measurable activity: contact, a record, a referral, temporary accommodation. The pledge here is framed around an offer, made to everyone, before Christmas. Mr Burnham's own words say the condition is a choice governments make, which is a claim about outcomes and not only about effort. The volunteer's account shows what an offer meets on the ground: people who have been outside for years, a council that opens extra beds when it freezes, and some who decline.

The test the manuscript would apply is whether the money ends in an exit. The report does not yet say. It says only what has been promised and what has been offered.
