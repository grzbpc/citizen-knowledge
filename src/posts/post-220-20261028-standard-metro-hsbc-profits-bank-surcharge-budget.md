### The story

A top HSBC executive has dismissed speculation that the group may quit the UK amid reports the chancellor could raise bank taxes in today's Budget. HSBC reported pre-tax profit of $9.6 billion (£7.2 billion) for the three months to September, 32 per cent higher than a year earlier and driven by higher interest rates. It also announced up to £750 million for shareholders in a new share buyback scheme. Barclays reported a 12 per cent jump in quarterly profit last week.

There are reports that John Healey could increase the surcharge on bank profits from 3 per cent to 8 per cent, reversing a tax cut made by the previous Conservative government. Asked whether higher taxes could see HSBC move its headquarters, chief financial officer Pam Kaur said "we are domiciled in the UK and we remain in the UK so that question is just not on the table". She said there was "no pressure because we are doing well, we're meeting all our targets". Industry groups have warned that higher taxes on financial services could divert investment, jobs and businesses away from the UK. Paul Nowak of the TUC said HSBC's profits made it "blatantly obvious banks can afford to contribute more", and the TUC said an 8 per cent surcharge would raise £9 billion over four years. Sara Hall of Positive Money accused banks of "blowing billions of pounds" on bonuses and payouts and called for a windfall tax.

Mr Healey is reportedly rejecting pressure to raise capital gains tax. Fuel duty is also set to be frozen for the 16th year in succession at 52p per litre. The chancellor has promised to fill a £4.7 billion hole in the defence investment plan and will commit £600 million to the development and safe use of AI, more than £1 billion to social and affordable housing, and £432 million to initiatives combating rough sleeping.

### The reframe

This entry takes no position on bank taxation, which is a contested question and one the report presents from several sides. It follows the sequence the page records. There are reports of a decision before it is made, a denial from a company that the decision would change where it is based, a warning from industry, and a response from campaigners with their own figure. Each statement is made to be read alongside the others, and the Budget itself comes after.

The manuscript says that modern politics rewards announcement over completion, and that the rewards of a declaration arrive at once while the practical results come later, if at all. Budget day is the clearest case. A set of figures is published together, each with a purpose attached: £600 million for AI, £432 million for rough sleeping, a £4.7 billion defence gap. The report gives the numbers and not what they will buy.

The one place where the reporting is concrete is the company's own result. A profit of £7.2 billion and a £750 million buyback are published facts, and they have been used by both sides: as a reason the bank can bear a higher charge, and as the basis for the bank's statement that it is under no pressure. What the tax will do is not yet known.
