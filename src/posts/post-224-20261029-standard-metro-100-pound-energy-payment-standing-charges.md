### The story

In his Budget, Chancellor John Healey announced a one-off £100 Household Energy Payment for around 20 million households in council tax bands A to D in England, paid directly into bank accounts in January at a cost of £2.6 billion. He also extended the VAT cut on electricity to July 2027, and said standing charges on gas bills would be scrapped next year. The Treasury said the standing charge change could save 60% of households an average of £45 a year. The chancellor said the payment would give "breathing space" on energy bills.

Campaigners said it was not enough. Simon Francis, co-ordinator of the End Fuel Poverty Coalition, said: "Wholesale gas prices have tripled, yet the only immediate support is a payment that will barely dent the predicted rise in January." He added that because it is paid into household accounts, "it may not even be spent on staying warm." Metro reports that Ofgem's price cap rose 3.6% to £1,723 for a typical household on 1 October, with a further rise forecast in January, put at around 22% in one report and at 27% in the Budget front page report.

Metro's guide also says that where standing charges are replaced by higher costs for each unit used, the change could leave larger and less energy efficient homes paying more.

### The reframe

The entry sets two numbers next to each other. One is the payment, £100. The other is the forecast rise that campaigners say it will barely dent. Metro reports both, and the gap between them is the matter in hand.

The manuscript has a chapter on the way announcements work in politics. It says announcements are immediate and visible, and that the practical conditions behind a problem change more slowly. It also says that the public learns to read an announcement as a sign that a problem exists. The payment fits the description in one respect: it can be named, it has a figure and a date, and it reaches a large number of households. Whether it solves the problem it is announced against is a separate question, and Mr Francis's answer is that it does not.

The standing charge change is the more instructive part. A standing charge is a fixed daily cost, and scrapping it is a visible gain that Metro's guide says is offset by higher charges for each unit. Households that use a lot of energy can come out worse. The headline measure and the settled effect on a given household are two different things, and which one a reader meets first depends on where the page puts it.

The entry draws no conclusion about the chancellor's intentions. A payment of £100 to 20 million households is real money, and Metro reports that it will arrive. The manuscript's point is narrower. When the stated aim is relief and the forecast is larger than the relief, the announcement tells the public how large the pressure is. Mr Francis's description of the commitments beyond this winter as "lukewarm at best" is the campaigners' reading, and the entry records it as theirs.
