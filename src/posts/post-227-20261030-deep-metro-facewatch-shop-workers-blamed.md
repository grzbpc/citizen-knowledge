### The story

Metro reports, in an exclusive, that minimum-wage shop workers are being forced to take the blame for failures of facial recognition cameras at some of the largest stores in the UK, MPs claim. The cameras, operated by Facewatch, scan the faces of people as they enter shops including Sainsbury's, Home Bargains and Sports Direct. The images are compared with a database of people flagged as shoplifters, which is compiled by retailers and their workers. MPs say workers have been put through disciplinary processes when innocent customers face theft allegations.

Metro gives three examples. A mother was wrongly added to the database after staff thought she had stolen a pair of trainers. A man was ordered to leave a Sainsbury's after staff mistook him for a different person who had been flagged. In both of those cases Facewatch said the error was down to shop workers not using the system correctly. In the third, a former police officer visiting a Budgens in Milton Keynes was told by a young female member of staff that she would have to leave because she had stolen a load of meat the week before. She and her husband asked for evidence, and after a review the worker admitted she had made a mistake. The former officer described the worker as a small young woman with no body-worn camera and no stab vest.

Dr Allison Gardner MP, co-chair of the Artificial Intelligence Parliamentary Group, told Metro that "Shop workers are being told to carry the failures of larger companies". She said the cause could be too little training or a mistake in the technology. Silkie Carlo of Big Brother Watch said there should be stronger control, or an outright ban. A Facewatch spokesperson said the company was shocked by Dr Gardner's comments, and that they appeared to dismiss the violence and abuse that industry bodies report against retail workers every day.

### The reframe, with the manuscript

The cameras do not accuse anyone. They return a match, and a person in a shop decides what to do with it. That is where Metro's story sits, and it is where the manuscript's account of classification begins to apply. A face is compared with a list, the comparison produces a result, and the result reaches the door or the till as something a member of staff has to act on within seconds, with a customer standing in front of them.

The manuscript describes what happens to the person on the receiving end of that kind of result.

> *The result is an environment in which categorisation increasingly precedes explanation. People are interpreted before they are understood.*

In the cases Metro reports, the customer is interpreted first. A woman is placed on a database because staff believed she had taken trainers. A man is asked to leave because he resembles someone else who was flagged. A former police officer is told she stole meat she had not stolen, and is cleared only because she and her husband insisted on seeing the evidence. In each case the explanation came afterwards, and had to be demanded by the person who had already been classified. The entry does not say how often this happens. Metro's report gives no figure for how many matches are wrong, and Facewatch's position on the earlier two cases is that they were errors of use, not errors of the system. What the cases show is the order of events: categorisation first, explanation second.

The other half of the story is about who carries the cost when the order goes wrong. Dr Gardner gives two possible causes, training or technology. Facewatch gives one, which is staff not using the system correctly. Both could be true, and the entry takes no view on which is right in any single case. They agree on where the problem lands, and it lands on the worker. The manuscript has a sentence for the direction this takes.

> *Failure is then attributed to individuals rather than design.*

It also describes who is left holding the contradiction when a system cannot be applied evenly.

> *The burden of coherence is pushed downward.*

That sentence is written about police officers, and a shop is a different setting. The shape is the same. A system produces results that are not always right, the people who operate it are not the people who built it, and the person who has to turn a flagged face into a decision is on the minimum wage, working without the protection the former officer noted was missing. The description of that exposure comes from a customer, not from the company or a union, but it states the position plainly.

Facewatch's reply should not be waved away. Violence and abuse against retail workers are real, and the company says critics should not overlook the people the cameras are meant to protect. Metro does not dispute that, and neither does this entry. The cameras exist because theft and aggression in shops are real. The point is narrower. The same system that is offered to protect the worker is, in the cases reported here, the thing that exposes the worker to blame, while the customer who has been wrongly flagged has to prove a negative at the door.

The same pattern appeared in the entry of 19 August on permanent cameras on Oxford Street, which also noted a retailer suspending its own facial recognition after wrongly accusing a customer. This is a further instance. The technology changes who is classified, and the cost of its errors lands on whoever happens to be standing nearest to it.
