### The story

Two reports on page 7 of Metro describe the same technology arriving at either end of a working life. In the first, research by GoStudent, in its Future of Education Report 2026, found that nearly three quarters of children who use AI at school admit passing off AI-generated work as their own. Around 73 per cent did not acknowledge AI's role in their work, but only 28 per cent had received a structured lesson on how to use the technology properly. Of the pupils who use AI, 58 per cent say they hand in work as their own at least once a week, which Metro says is the highest of the six European countries polled. And 94 per cent of teachers said they are aware that pupils do it.

In the second, a survey of 928 managers by the Chartered Management Institute found that the use of AI by job applicants is moving beyond written applications and into the interview itself. Almost three in five said they had noticed or suspected a candidate using AI during an online interview. Some 90 per cent said candidates were using AI to help write CVs or cover letters, up from 79 per cent two years ago. Half said they worried a candidate may be misrepresenting their skills or experience, and the same proportion said it made them less confident in the candidate's ability. Petra Wilton of the CMI asked, "Are we interviewing a person or ChatGPT?", and said candidates, especially early in their careers, need to think about how to build their own communication and critical-thinking skills.

### The reframe, with the manuscript

Neither report says that anyone has been punished, and the entry does not say that anyone should be. Passing a machine's work off as your own is a problem for a school, and misleading an employer is a problem for a hiring manager. The point here is narrower, and it concerns how the rule gets taught.

The manuscript begins its account of this with a claim about where people learn how a system works.

> *People do not learn how systems function from the institutions that claim to teach them.*

The GoStudent figures sit in the gap that sentence describes. The rule that schoolwork should be a pupil's own already exists. What Metro reports is that most pupils who use AI do not say so, that fewer than three in ten have been shown how to use it properly, and that 94 per cent of teachers say they are aware of it. The instruction is largely missing, and the behaviour is visible to the people who set the rule. The entry does not claim that schools allow it; Metro reports no survey of what pupils believe the rule to be, or of what teachers do when they see it. What the figures are consistent with, though they do not prove it, is the process the manuscript describes next.

> *Formal instruction presents rules as universal. Experience reveals that their application depends on context, status, and discretion.*

The employers' figures show the same process from the other side, and they show a boundary being moved while nobody states where it is. Ninety per cent of managers say candidates use AI on their CVs and cover letters, up eleven points in two years, and the survey treats this as the setting in which the new concern arises rather than as the concern itself. The worry is reserved for the interview, where managers say they are no longer getting a true picture of the person they are hiring. Metro does not say where acceptable help ends and misrepresentation begins, and nothing in the report suggests that applicants were told. The advice Metro quotes tells candidates that reliance on AI may undermine their chances, which leaves the responsibility for working out the line with them.

A pupil who has used AI throughout school, with no structured lesson, arrives at an interview where the same habit has become a risk to their chances. The manuscript's description of what school rewards bears on that route.

> *Success becomes a matter of tolerating the process rather than understanding the material.*

That sentence is written about education before any of this technology existed, and it should not be stretched. It does name the condition under which a tool that produces finished work is attractive, and it sits alongside Ms Wilton's closing advice, to build critical thinking and communication skills, as a reminder that the skills she names are the ones a finished-work tool does not exercise. The entry draws no conclusion about any individual pupil or candidate. It records that, on Metro's figures, the people using the tool were rarely taught how, and that the people judging the result are now asking who they are dealing with.
