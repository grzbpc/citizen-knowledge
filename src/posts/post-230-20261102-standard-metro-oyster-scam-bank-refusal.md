### The story

Metro's front page reports that police have arrested a gang of suspected scammers who rigged Oyster card machines at Tube stations to steal thousands of pounds from travellers. The thieves are believed to have doctored the machines to skim debit card details as passengers topped up their cards. British Transport Police has launched Operation Solent over reports of fraudulent transactions on some ticket machines, and says enquiries are continuing.

The report is built around one victim, Étienne Bouvier, a software worker from Lille who works for a firm with an office in London. He says his contactless payment was rejected several times by a TfL machine at Piccadilly Circus, so he switched to a chip and pin device instead. Two days later he found two cash withdrawals of £800 each at bank machines in the City of London, made while he was on a Eurostar train home to France. By the following Monday, he says, he was missing nearly £2,000. Other victims report £500 taken after topping up at St Paul's, and £250 after a transaction at Piccadilly Circus. Metro says some victims were reimbursed by their bank. Mr Bouvier says his bank refused, and claimed he had failed to adequately protect his card details.

A TfL spokesperson said the safety and security of customers is a top priority, that the network regularly inspects its ticketing equipment, and that customers should report anything suspicious to station staff or the police. Detective Inspector Marvin Bruno of British Transport Police urged passengers to "remain vigilant when using ticket machines, especially if they function strangely". Metro adds that visitors using foreign bank cards can be more vulnerable to skimmers, because they are often required to make a chip and pin transaction first before contactless payments can be enabled.

### The reframe

The report gives two separate accounts of where the responsibility sits. The police and TfL describe a crime committed against passengers, carried out through ticket machines that TfL says it regularly inspects. The bank, on Mr Bouvier's account of it, describes a customer who did not look after his own card.

Three details in Metro's report make that second position hard to square with the first. The machine that rejected his card several times is the sort of strange behaviour the police now ask passengers to watch for, and his response was to use chip and pin, which is the route Metro says foreign card holders are often required to take. The report does not say whether Mr Bouvier saw the rejections as a warning; it says only what he did next. The same report says that other victims of the same operation were reimbursed. Metro does not say how many, or why the outcomes differed, and the bank is not quoted, so the entry has only Mr Bouvier's account of the bank's reasoning and does not say the refusal was wrong.

What can be set down is the order of the decisions. Where the outcome turns on a bank's judgement of whether a customer took enough care, results can differ between people caught by the same device, and the explanation, when one is given, rests on the customer's behaviour. The manuscript describes this shape in its account of threshold enforcement: the institution keeps the option of a decisive response, while the cost of any uncertainty falls on the person the rule is applied to. Here the uncertainty is a compromised machine that a passenger could not easily have inspected, and the standard of care the customer is expected to meet is not set out anywhere in Metro's report.

The police advice to be vigilant is reasonable, and nothing here suggests it is offered in bad faith. It does show where the burden is placed in practice. A passenger is asked to notice what a rigged machine does, and a customer who is later refunded or refused is, in effect, judged on whether he noticed enough. The report does not say whether Mr Bouvier's bank has reviewed its decision since he spoke to Metro.
