### The story

Metro reports on page 5 that Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood has watered down her plans to make foreign care workers wait up to 15 years for settlement, after criticism from senior Labour figures. Under her initial proposals, lower-qualified workers who arrived on health and social care visas from 2022, in what the paper calls the "Boriswave", could have been made to wait 15 years for permanent settlement. Under the revised plans, foreign care workers who meet their visa conditions and earn at least £25,000 a year will now wait five years, Ms Mahmood told the Commons, and those who do not qualify will be able to apply for a new five-year visa.

Metro says the change spares up to 300,000 care workers the longer wait, and that the care sector roundly welcomed it. Housing Secretary Angela Rayner had attacked proposals to backdate the changes. Ms Mahmood also said that the right to settled status would no longer be automatic and must be earned, with a fast-track for high earners and some public service workers. She told the Commons that the Conservatives had allowed "significant abuse of the care route", and that the new rules addressed this while allowing care workers who lived up to the promise they made on coming to the country to settle. Shadow Home Secretary Chris Philp pointed to government estimates on allowing overseas care workers and their dependants to settle, and called it "the cost of today's U-turn", at £10 billion. Metro adds that the Institute for Public Policy Research said skilled migrants and their dependants would contribute £115 billion to the economy.

### The reframe, with the manuscript

The entry does not take a view on whether the revised rule is right, on the £10 billion estimate, or on the IPPR figure. Metro reports both, and they cannot be reconciled from the page. The narrower point concerns what the rule measures.

A care worker's route to permanent settlement now depends on a pay figure. The visa is the same, the conditions are the same, and the job is the same; what separates a five-year wait from a new five-year visa is whether earnings reach £25,000. The same announcement describes settled status as something to be earned, with a faster route for high earners. The manuscript begins its account of this with a sentence about what employment is taken to show.

> *In societies that moralise work, employment becomes a proxy for character.*

Metro does not report what share of care workers fall below the line, or why that figure was chosen, so the entry cannot say how many people end up on the second track. It records only that the government's own language ties status to being earned, and that the measure of earning is a salary. Ms Mahmood's remark about workers who lived up to the promise they made on arriving is a statement about conduct, but the test in the revised rule, as Metro reports it, is an amount of money.

The second feature is that the line moved. The original proposal was reported as a 15-year wait for lower-qualified workers; the revised one is five years above a pay figure, and Metro attributes the revision to criticism from senior Labour figures, without reporting any new evidence about care workers behind it. The manuscript's description of thresholds is of boundaries that are usually unstated.

> *The threshold remains unseen and movable, and behaviour bends around it accordingly.*

This threshold is published, so the first half of that sentence does not apply, and the entry does not claim that it does. The second half does apply: the figure was not fixed, it was revised under pressure, and anyone planning around it, whether a care worker or an employer, now has to plan around the revised version. Whether the revision is a correction of an overreach or a concession to a lobby is exactly what the competing figures in Metro's report are disputing, and the entry leaves that question where Metro leaves it.
