### The story

Metro reports on page 3 that more than a quarter of UK employees have considered quitting over what it calls "loud working", meaning colleagues bragging about their achievements, and that nearly one in ten have become so fed up with the boasts that they handed in their notice. Workers spend 131 hours a year making their work visible, the report says, including by pointing out accomplishments to bosses or posting on LinkedIn. Jim Moore, an employee relations expert at Hamilton Nash, told Metro that it can be a sign of a "worrying" workplace culture. He said the need for constant self-promotion suggests that business leaders value performative productivity over good work, and that "Quieter colleagues who simply get on with it feel punished." The report as printed does not name the survey behind the figures.

### The reframe

On a 52-week year, 131 hours averages a little over two and a half hours a week, spent not on the work but on being seen to have done it. That is the figure the entry rests on, with the caveat that the report does not say how it was measured or who was asked.

The manuscript's account of work begins from a distinction between activity and usefulness: where outcomes are hard to measure, organisations fall back on visible substitutes, and the people who supply those substitutes advance. The substitute the manuscript describes most fully is endurance, with long hours and visible strain taken as proof of commitment. Loud working is a different signal, since it is announced achievement rather than visible strain, and the entry does not treat the two as the same thing. What they share is the mechanism, in that something other than the work itself becomes the thing that is noticed.

Mr Moore's remark puts the same point from the receiving end. He locates the cause in what business leaders value, not in the individuals who boast, and he reports the cost as falling on the quieter colleague. That is one expert's reading, and Metro gives no evidence about how any particular employer rewards or ignores self-promotion, so the entry offers it as consistent with the manuscript's argument and not as proof of it. It is also worth noting that the employees in the survey who say they are fed up are describing the behaviour of colleagues, not of managers; the figures say nothing about whether the people who set the rewards agree with the diagnosis.

Read this way, the story is less about vanity than about incentive. If standing at work depends partly on being seen to have achieved, then the 131 hours are a rational use of time for the individual and a cost to the organisation, and the colleague who declines to join in is the one who bears it. The manuscript's phrase for what results is moral theatre; the report offers a number for how much of it there is.
