### The story

Metro reports on page 5 that MI5 and its director general have avoided legal action after the secret service provided false evidence to three courts. High Court judges ruled that prosecution "would not be in the public interest", the paper says. The incorrect evidence was used four years ago in a successful application for an injunction that prevented the BBC naming a covert intelligence source who allegedly abused two women.

Metro reports that MI5 maintained for three years that the information was accurate, and later admitted that it was wrong, with the director general, Sir Ken McCallum, apologising. A hearing in September was told that MI5 and individuals including Sir Ken should face contempt charges, and lawyers for one of the women claimed that there had been repeated efforts to prevent the truth emerging. The paper does not report the judges' reasoning, who brought the application, or whether the ruling can be challenged.

### The reframe, with the manuscript

The entry does not take a view on whether the judges were right. Public interest is a legal test, Metro gives no account of how it was applied, and there may be considerations the page leaves out. The narrower point is the shape of the outcome as reported: a state body supplied evidence that it later admitted to be false, the evidence helped to secure a court order, the body maintained for three years that it was accurate, and the consequence recorded is an apology and a ruling against prosecution.

The manuscript begins its account of sanctions from a different question, which is what a penalty actually measures.

> *Punishment no longer teaches norms; it teaches positioning.*

On that account a sanction follows not the seriousness of the conduct but whether the person or body concerned has insulation. Here the body is the security service of the state itself, and the reported result is that no penalty follows. Metro's paragraph cannot show that insulation explains the ruling, so the entry offers the pattern as consistent with the argument and not as proof of it. Contempt of court exists to protect the integrity of what courts are told, and the paper reports that it was argued for at a hearing and not applied.

The same page carries a second report that the manuscript's method makes hard to ignore. A man from Wigan has been given a 12-month suspended prison sentence for stealing stones worth £7,000 from a historic wall, after an amateur detective hid a tracker in a loose stone and police traced the stones to his yard. The two reports concern different conduct, different courts and different legal tests, and the entry does not suggest that one should have been handled as the other was. It records only that they sit together on one page. The manuscript makes the same observation about a single week's news in 2012.

> *Neither report referenced the other. The two registers of enforcement operated side by side, in public, without comment.*

Metro's reports do not refer to each other either. What the page does not say is what remedy, if any, follows for the two women, and that is the part of the story that the apology leaves open. The manuscript describes what people learn from a gap of this kind.

> *People grow accustomed to the gap between stated rules and applied rules. They stop expecting consistency and begin expecting navigation.*

The entry does not claim that any reader will draw that conclusion from one paragraph. It notes that the reported gap is between a rule on false evidence and its application to the body that supplied it, and that Metro's account stops at the ruling.
