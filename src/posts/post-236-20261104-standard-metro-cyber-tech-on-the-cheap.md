### The story

Metro's front page and page 7 report that Dr Richard Horne, chief executive of the National Cyber Security Centre, which is part of GCHQ, says the gangs that sabotaged supermarkets across Europe have already struck in Britain. On the day of the report, UK meat processors said their factories had also been damaged in attacks. On Sunday, stores in seven other countries had freezers switched to thaw remotely, ruining tonnes of food. The British Association of Independent Meat Suppliers said some of its members were collateral damage, with two having fridges defrost and a third reaching 100C.

Dr Horne warns of a real prospect of an unprecedented rise in cyber incidents, and says the cause is not that artificial intelligence has invented new methods of attack but that weaknesses left in place for years can now be found and exploited at a scale once unimaginable. He says organisations have collectively got away with underinvesting in technology for decades, with upgrades delayed to save money, and that business leaders who fail to act will have to explain to investors, customers and families why they did not make the choices required. He also backed the Be Ready campaign, which urges Britons to keep emergency kits. Metro's headline carries party-political criticism of the government, which this entry does not assess.

### The reframe

The entry does not assess the scale of the attacks, which Metro reports only through the official's account and one trade body. The narrower point is what the official says about the exposure itself: that it is long-standing, that it was known, and that it is the product of decisions about cost and not of any new skill on the attackers' side.

The manuscript's chapter on failure begins from the observation that resolution is difficult while administration is always possible, and that real resolution would mean confronting incentives far upstream. A delayed upgrade is a plain example. Each year of delay saves money and nothing visibly goes wrong, until a threshold is crossed and the same exposure is described as new. Dr Horne's distinction between an old weakness and a new scale is a description of that sequence, given by someone with the standing to know.

The second feature is where the reported response lands. Metro records advice to households to keep emergency kits, and a warning to business leaders that they will have to give an account of themselves later. The manuscript describes failure being managed at the edges instead of owned at the centre, with responsibility deferred, rotated or reclassified. The first measure reported is directed at individuals and the second is an expectation of future explanation, and Metro reports no step that closes any particular weakness, though one may exist and simply not be on the page.

One fact cuts against the simple reading. The manuscript's pattern concerns tolerance of a known failure, and it observes that institutions rarely say openly that something does not work. Here a senior official is saying so in public, and in plain terms. The entry therefore records a partial fit, in which the tolerance is acknowledged and the open question is what follows from the acknowledgement. Later reporting will show whether anything beyond advice and warning does.
