### The story

Metro reports on page 9 that cocaine has overtaken heroin as the substance involved in most drug-related deaths for the first time, according to the Office for National Statistics. Some 1,233 deaths involved cocaine, against 1,210 involving heroin or morphine. Cocaine deaths fell from 1,279 in 2024, but are eight times higher than in 2010, when there were 144. Deaths linked to the synthetic cannabinoid Spice, often consumed by prisoners, rose from 44 to 95. Experts warn that young people are taking the drug by vaping, and that "do it yourself" assembly kits costing £1,000, which can produce a kilogram of Spice, are being imported into the UK.

The paper reports that the synthetic opioid nitazene was responsible for 150 deaths in England and Wales, down from 195, but that Dr Caroline Copeland of King's College London warns the true figure may be much higher because the drug is very difficult to detect. Mike Trace, an ex-government drug czar who heads the Forward Trust, says the threat on the ground is not reflected in the figures. Overall there were 5,519 drug-poisoning deaths last year, down from 5,565 in 2024, which the ONS describes as the first drop in the mortality rate in 13 years. The charity WithYou says cocaine has become "more socially acceptable and cheaper" in recent years. Mark Brooks of the Centre for Policy Research on Men and Boys calls the data a "public health emergency" and says far more must be done on root causes such as lack of employment, mental health and isolation.

Beneath it, a separate item reports that a dealer who ran a "takeaway" service for crack and heroin has been jailed for four years and nine months, after a clip of him asking a buyer through a window for his order helped convict him.

### The reframe, with the manuscript

The entry does not assess the ONS figures, which Metro gives in summary, or the experts' warnings that some of the toll goes uncounted. It records what the page puts side by side. A report that cocaine, described by a charity as socially acceptable, now sits at the top of the death table has beneath it a report of a supplier whose service was visible enough to be filmed and punished. The two cases are unconnected and the paper does not link them, so nothing here suggests the sentence was out of proportion. The pairing is the point, and the manuscript describes the structure it shows.

> *Drugs are prohibited in principle but tolerated in practice, up to a point. That point is defined not by harm, but by visibility, insulation, and class.*

Two features of the report cut against a simple reading. The headline rank is not a rise: cocaine deaths fell year on year, and the change is that heroin deaths are lower still. Overall deaths also fell slightly, for the first time in 13 years. The page therefore shows a harm that is large and shifting, and not one that is plainly growing, and the entry does not claim otherwise.

What the page does not report is any response from government, and that absence matters for the manuscript's second point, which concerns how enforcement is judged.

> *Arrests, seizures, and sentences stand in for outcomes. Success is measured in activity rather than impact.*

Metro's sentencing item is activity of exactly that kind, a conviction secured and a term served, and the death figures above it are the outcome. The paper does not say how, or whether, one affects the other, and the experts it quotes point elsewhere, to employment, mental health and isolation. That is consistent with the manuscript's account of a policy that is judged by what is done and not by what changes, though a single page cannot show that this is how success is measured in practice.

The chapter's closing observation on drug policy is that failure is managed.

> *As with welfare and homelessness, failure is contained rather than resolved.*

The page does not show containment. It shows a figure that fell slightly, two experts saying it understates the problem and a third calling it an emergency. Whether anything beyond enforcement follows from it is the open question, and it is one that Metro's report leaves unanswered.
