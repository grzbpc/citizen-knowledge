### The story

Metro reports on pages 24 and 25 that Avram Glazer, co-owner of Manchester United, has claimed that the club's value is not dependent on success on the pitch. He was speaking at Deutsche Bank's inaugural Sports Investment Conference in London, in September, and the remarks have only just come to light. Asked by an audience member whether there is a clear link between on-pitch glory and commercial success, he answered forcefully. According to The Athletic, as relayed by Metro, he likened the club to franchises such as the Dallas Cowboys and the New York Yankees, which have not won the Super Bowl or the World Series for years.

Metro sets this beside the club's position. United have not won the league since 2013, when Sir Alex Ferguson left, and are 17th, just above the relegation zone, ahead of Sunday's home game against Aston Villa. The paper says relegation, though unlikely, would be financially devastating. Commercial revenue was £152 million in 2012-13, £276 million in 2017-18 and a reported £317 million for 2025-26. Metro also records that performance has cost the club some standing: Forbes ranked United third most valuable in May, behind Real Madrid and Barcelona, where a year earlier only Real Madrid were ahead. Debt is described as the centre of supporters' frustration, with the Glazers having leveraged bank money to take over in 2005 and total debt at £689 million as recently as June. Ineos is a minority owner, and Sir Jim Ratcliffe's team has made football decisions since February 2024, while Avram and Joel Glazer remain central to budgets and other key decisions.

### The reframe, with the manuscript

The entry does not take a view on whether Mr Glazer is right. Metro gives his answer second-hand, through another outlet, from an event two months old, and it does not report his exact words. A claim made to investors about a club's commercial strength may well be accurate about revenue. The narrower point is what the claim says about where value is understood to sit, and what the paper's own figures show beside it.

The manuscript's chapter on markets begins from the public story of investment, in which value follows productive performance, and argues that the link has loosened.

> *The issue is that the public justification for markets, investment as participation in productive growth, no longer describes how large parts of the system operate.*

A football club is not a listed company, and the chapter concerns financial markets, so the entry offers the parallel as a parallel and not as an instance. Within that limit, the reported claim has the same shape. On the owner's account, the club's worth rests on the scale of its brand across the world, which is a measure of audience and attention, and results on the pitch are not the thing being valued. The page's figures are at least consistent with that account: commercial revenue is reported as roughly double what it was around the time of the last title, though the figures are nominal and the paper does not adjust them. The manuscript describes the general pattern.

> *This echoes the pattern seen in welfare, education, and enforcement: the system's survival does not depend on the outcomes it claims to produce. It depends on continued participation.*

Two features of the page cut against a simple reading. The first is the Forbes ranking, which Metro says fell from second to third over a year, so the page does not show value fully detached from results; it shows a partial separation, and the paper itself names relegation as the point at which results would clearly reach the accounts. The second is the debt. Metro links the figure to supporters' frustration and to the 2005 takeover but does not say how it is serviced or who bears the cost, so the entry draws nothing further from it.

The manuscript is explicit that a divergence of this kind is not an accusation.

> *This is not evidence of corruption. It is evidence of misalignment. The mechanism continues to function, but its public explanation no longer fits.*

That is the sense in which the entry records the story. Nothing on the page suggests wrongdoing, and the owner's remark may simply be an accurate description of how the business earns money. What the page leaves open is how supporters, who are told that a club is its team, should read an account from the person at the centre of its budgets in which the team is not what the value depends on.
