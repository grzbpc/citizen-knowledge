### The story

A short item on Metro's page 4 reports that employers will be legally required to include salary information on job adverts, as announced by Andy Burnham. The prime minister said, "You should know what a job pays" before spending time and effort applying for it. The paper adds that the CIPD found 27% of employers had never listed salaries.

### The reframe

The entry does not assess the policy, which is a requirement many applicants would welcome and which narrows a plain gap in what each side knows. The narrower question is what the paragraph contains and what it leaves out.

The manuscript's chapter on symbolic politics describes a political culture in which announcing is rewarded more reliably than completing, because an announcement is immediate and visible while enforcement is slow and rarely noticed. On that account, a statement of intent becomes evidence of commitment before the conditions for delivery exist. The paragraph carries a statement of intent and one survey figure. It gives no date for the requirement to begin, no penalty for an employer that omits a salary, no body responsible for checking adverts and no indication of how a salary range, a bonus or a figure described as competitive would be treated. Some of these may be settled elsewhere, and the item is only a few lines long, so the absence is a limit of the page and not a finding.

Two things cut against the simple reading. A legal duty is a stronger instrument than a target or a consultation, and the manuscript's concern is with announcements that substitute for action, not with every announcement. The survey figure also helps the case for the measure: if more than a quarter of employers never list pay, the gap is real. The entry therefore records a partial fit. What would settle it is whether the requirement is enforced, by whom, and against whom, which later reporting may show.
