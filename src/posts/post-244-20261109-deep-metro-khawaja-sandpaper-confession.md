### The story

A Sport in brief item on Metro's page 24 reports that Usman Khawaja says Steve Smith talked him out of the Australia team taking collective responsibility for the 2018 sandpaper scandal. Mr Khawaja, who is promoting his autobiography, claims that all players and staff knew about the ball-tampering in South Africa.

The paper recalls what happened on the field and afterwards. Cameras caught Cameron Bancroft using sandpaper during the third Test in Cape Town. Mr Smith, the captain, and David Warner, his deputy, were banned for a year, and Mr Bancroft for nine months. Mr Khawaja says that he told Mr Smith he was going to come clean, in the words Metro gives him, "I told Smudge I was going to come clean", and that Mr Smith talked him out of it. Asked why he is saying this now, he said he was always conscious of putting his team first, but that he is not in that team any more.

### The reframe, with the manuscript

The entry does not decide whether Mr Khawaja's account is true. Metro gives it in a few paragraphs of a sports roundup, as the claim of a player promoting a book, and the item carries no response from Mr Smith, Mr Warner, Mr Bancroft or anyone else named. The bans, by contrast, are a matter of record, and they fell on three men with clear roles: the player seen on camera, the captain and his deputy. A penalty scaled to role is an ordinary way of apportioning responsibility, so the existence of three sanctions does not in itself show that anyone was treated unevenly. The narrower question is what the page makes visible and what it leaves out.

The manuscript's chapter on selective enforcement begins from the observation that consequences can depend on position and on being seen, more than on conduct alone.

> *What matters is not the behaviour itself, but the circumstances surrounding it; who is visible, who is expendable, and who is insulated.*

On the page, the visible fact is a camera. Mr Bancroft was caught on it, and the bans that followed are set out in the paper's own figures. What the item does not say is how the wider group came to be examined or not examined, or what the body that imposed the bans knew about anyone else. Mr Khawaja's claim, if accurate, would mean that a much larger group held knowledge of the plan and that only three were sanctioned. The entry takes care over that distinction: knowing about an act is not the same as carrying it out, and nothing on the page suggests that the others did more than he says they knew.

The manuscript describes how a sanction of this kind can look orderly from the outside.

> *The rule existed. The behaviour crossed a line. The response followed. What disappears from view is the discretionary space in between; the period during which identical behaviour was tolerated elsewhere or earlier.*

Mr Khawaja's own account places part of that in-between space inside the team. By his telling, the question of whether a wider group would share responsibility was settled in a conversation between players, and the choice to stay quiet was made by him on the advice of his captain, not imposed by an authority. That is a different thing from selective enforcement by an institution, and the entry does not treat it as one. It does show how the outcome the public saw, three bans, could have been produced by decisions the public was never shown.

What the page cannot settle is whether any of this is accurate and, if it is, whether it would have changed the sanctions. The account is a single person's, offered at a promotional moment and years after the event. A response from those he names, or any later reporting on who was asked what at the time, would be what turns a claim into a finding.
