### The story

An item on Metro's page 5 reports that Danielle Hand, 30, a self-employed carer from Doncaster, is demanding that her children's primary school stop rewarding pupils with a 100% attendance record. Her son, five, and daughter, seven, were made to sit and watch classmates eat hot chocolate and cake after they missed four days of school for a family holiday. Ms Hand says she could not afford the trip in the summer, because it cost too much.

She wrote in a Facebook post, which the paper says was viewed a million times, "Attendance should not be used as a weapon to not reward a child. It is a form of bullying." She has also launched a petition. Her local MP, Ed Miliband, said he had "noted her concerns", and the school is investigating.

### The reframe

The entry does not judge the school or the mother. A reward for attendance is a plain and widely used way of encouraging pupils to turn up, and the school's own account is not in the item, which says only that it is investigating. The narrower question is what the arrangement, as Metro describes it, teaches.

The manuscript's chapter on education argues that schools transmit expectations about which rules matter, and that the lesson is carried less by what is said than by what is rewarded. It describes a system in which success is praised for endurance and persistence, and where the measure stands in for the thing it was meant to indicate. A perfect-attendance reward is a clean example of a measure that anyone can see and check. It records whether a child was present, and says nothing about what the child learned or how they were doing in class. On the page, the cost of that clarity fell on two children who missed four days, for a reason their mother links to the price of travelling in the summer.

Two features keep this a partial fit. The rule appears to have been stated and applied in the same way to every pupil, so the item shows no uneven enforcement, which is the manuscript's central concern. And a term-time holiday is a choice, whatever its cost, which a school may reasonably treat differently from illness. What the item does show is a rule that is universal in form and conditional in effect, since whether a family can avoid a missed day depends in part on what travel costs and when. That is the pattern the manuscript describes, in a small and mild instance. What would settle how far it goes is the school's response to its investigation, and whether the reward changes.
