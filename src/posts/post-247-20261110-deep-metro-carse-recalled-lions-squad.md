### The story

A report on Metro's page 27 says that Brydon Carse, the England seamer, has been named in the Lions squads for the tour of South Africa, and that the national selector, Marcus North, has left the door open for him to feature in the Test series that follows. Mr Carse has not been selected in any international set-up since he was handcuffed outside a Derby nightclub in August. He was dropped for the second Test of England's 3-0 home win over Pakistan; the red-ball captain, Joe Root, admitted that the incident left him "hacked off", and the white-ball captain, Harry Brook, said that Mr Carse needed "time away".

Derbyshire Police investigated an alleged assault in connection with the incident and brought no charges. The regulator nonetheless deemed that Mr Carse, 31, had breached the England and Wales Cricket Board's professional conduct rules. Mr North described the past few months as "a time of reflection", and said that after several conversations between himself, the managing director Rob Key and others at the ECB, "we feel he is in a place where we can introduce him back into an England environment", with the Lions the right place for him. With Josh Tongue set to miss the first Test on 17 December because of the birth of his second child, Mr North said that "one or two players" from the Lions group will be drafted into the Test squad, and he did not rule out Mr Carse.

### The reframe, with the manuscript

The entry does not argue that Mr Carse has been treated too lightly or too harshly. On the page the sequence is graduated and recognisable: removal from one Test, a period outside the selected group, a finding by the regulator that conduct rules were breached, and a return that begins in the second-tier squad and not the first. No police charge followed, so the regulator's finding is the only formal outcome the paper reports. A sanction of that shape may well be proportionate. The narrower question is who describes each step, and in what terms.

The manuscript is not hostile to discretion as such. In its chapter on policing it says:

> *When exercised within a coherent framework, discretion can humanise enforcement. When exercised within a contradictory one, it becomes arbitrary.*

The recall reads, on its face, as the humane kind. A player who has had a finding made against him, been absent from international cricket for some months, and held conversations with the people who select him is being brought back by stages, and nothing on the page suggests that anything improper lies in that. What the page does not give is the framework. It does not say what the regulator found, which rule the finding rested on, whether any penalty attached to it, or what the test for a return is. The only account of the process is the selector's, in the phrases "a time of reflection" and "in a place where we can introduce him back"; those describe a judgement about the man's state, not a rule that was applied to him.

That matters because of what an earlier entry in this archive recorded. In August it set Mr Carse's case beside that of two other players whose night out ended with the regulator finding no case to answer. Today's report adds the other end of the sequence, which is the end the institution controls most closely. Selection is a decision the ECB makes in its own name, with no outside body obliged to explain it, and the road back can be as long or as short as those making the decision choose. The manuscript's concern with that arrangement is not that anyone acts in bad faith, but that the people subject to it are left to work out the pattern for themselves:

> *Citizens cannot orient their behaviour around principles that are applied unevenly and explained after the fact.*

For a professional cricketer, the people in that position are the other players. Whether a breach finding means one missed match or several months out of selection, whether a recall comes through the Lions or directly, and whether the answer turns on how much a bowler is needed in a particular series, are matters they can only infer from cases like this one. The page supplies one fact that bears on this: the opening in the Test group exists because Mr Tongue is unavailable, and Mr North presents the Lions tour as a way of using that to the England side's advantage. That does not show that need drove the decision, since every reason Mr North gives concerns Mr Carse himself. It does show that need and rehabilitation arrive together, and that an outside reader cannot separate them.

What the page cannot settle is whether the process was consistent. That would require the regulator's finding, its published reasons, and the treatment of other players found in breach of the same rules, and none of these is in the report.
