### The story

An item on Metro's page 2 reports that Andy Burnham has insisted that no decision has been made on changes to electric vehicle targets, amid reports that he is preparing to cut them by a quarter. Mr Burnham, who is attending this week's Cop31 climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, for the first time as prime minister, was asked during a visit to Watford how he would justify cutting EV sales while trying to reduce emissions. He said, "These reports are just that: they're reports, they're speculation and no decision has been made."

A government consultation on the zero emission vehicle mandate has recently concluded, and carmakers had raised concerns about an order to phase out petrol and diesel vehicles. The current rule requires 80% of car sales to be zero emission, usually electric-powered, by 2030, with hybrids allowed to make up the rest. Under the options set out in the consultation, the target could be cut to as low as 50%. The Guardian reported that the government is expected to sign off on 60%. The Society of Motor Manufacturers and Traders welcomed the review.

### The reframe

The entry does not judge whether the target should be lowered. A government consulting on a legal requirement, hearing from the industry that must meet it, and then revising it is an ordinary and open use of the process, and the paper reports no concealment. Mr Burnham's statement that nothing has been decided is not contradicted by anything on the page; the 60% figure is a newspaper's report of an expectation, and the item presents it as one.

The narrower question is what a stated target is worth while it remains open to negotiation by the people it binds. The manuscript's first chapter describes a rule that, once it bends to circumstance, stops working as a fixed commitment and starts working as leverage, with its practical meaning settled by pressure and not by the text. On the page the sequence is plain. The rule is set at 80%; those subject to it object; the consultation offers options as low as 50%; and the trade body for the sector welcomes the review. Each step is visible and lawful. What the sequence shows is that a figure written into the rule, and presented to the public as the commitment, was in practice a number with a range around it, and that the lower end of the range was shaped partly by the concerns of the regulated parties.

Two features keep this a partial fit. Nothing here concerns enforcement, which is the manuscript's central subject, since no manufacturer is reported as being treated differently from another. And the page gives only a clause on the pressure itself: it does not say what the carmakers' concerns were, what evidence supported them, or what was said by those who argued for keeping the target. That would be needed to judge whether the objections were weighed fairly or simply prevailed.

What would settle how far the pattern goes is the government's published response to the consultation, which should show what was proposed, who responded, and why the final figure, whatever it is, was chosen.
