### The story

Metro reports on page 33 that Mitchell Starc is sticking to his account of the 2018 ball-tampering scandal, and that Cricket Australia has ruled out a fresh inquiry. The affair is back in the news because of Usman Khawaja's new autobiography, covered in an earlier entry, in which he claims that the team had been tampering with the ball before the Cape Town Test where Cameron Bancroft was caught on camera, and that the entire squad knew. At the time Steve Smith and David Warner were banned for 12 months, and Mr Bancroft for nine months, a shorter period because of his rookie status.

Mr Starc, together with Pat Cummins, Josh Hazlewood and Nathan Lyon, released a statement in 2021 which Metro describes as carefully worded, denying any knowledge of the tampering. Speaking in Sydney, Mr Starc said that his recollection has not changed from that statement, and that as far as it went, the bowlers were not aware of sandpaper being taken onto the field until they saw it on the big screen in Cape Town. Asked whether everyone knew, he said that it is in a book and is for someone else to explain further. Mr Warner, one of the three who were banned, said on Monday that Mr Khawaja's account is completely accurate and that everyone now knows the truth.

Against those differing accounts, the Cricket Australia chief executive, Todd Greenberg, told SEN that the body does not see "any compelling reason to open the investigation or revisit this again". He added that it is comfortable with the outcome based on the information it has received, which he said includes what it has seen from Mr Khawaja. Metro sets beside this the former Australia captain Michael Clarke, who says he cannot believe that nothing has yet been heard from Mr Cummins, and that Australian supporters cannot trust what is real until the matter is reopened.

### The reframe, with the manuscript

This entry follows the one on Monday, which asked what the page made visible. Today's report adds a decision. The page sets out accounts from people who were there that do not agree, one of them from a player who was sanctioned, and a governing body that has heard them and concludes that nothing further is needed. The entry does not say who is right. Mr Khawaja's claim has the backing of Mr Warner but no one else is reported as confirming it, Mr Starc's position is consistent with what the bowlers said in 2021, and a body is entitled to decide that a matter which produced three bans eight years ago has been dealt with. Declining to reopen is a legitimate choice. The narrower question is what form the choice takes, and what it rests on.

Cricket Australia's stated ground is that it is comfortable with the outcome. That outcome was reached on the information available at the time, and the information now in dispute is precisely what the page describes as contested. A conclusion that rests on what was received earlier cannot by itself test whether what was received was complete. The statement says that the information it has considered includes what the body has seen from Mr Khawaja, but the page does not say what that was, or whether anyone else has been asked to say more.

The manuscript's chapter on symbolic politics closes with an account of why institutions find this difficult, and it is a general account, not a comment on any one body.

> *What it cannot do easily is correct itself. Correction requires acknowledgement. Acknowledgement requires honesty.*

On the page, acknowledgement is being offered by individuals, in a book and in a remark to the press, and what is absent is any route by which it could be received. That is a different thing from a body denying that anything happened. Cricket Australia does not deny the scandal, and it kept the original sanctions in place. What the report records is a decision not to open a process in which further acknowledgement could take place, taken on the strength of a view that the earlier outcome still stands.

The same chapter describes what can follow when a body relies on the standing of its earlier conclusions.

> *Eventually legitimacy begins resting less upon consent than inertia.*

Mr Clarke's remark, that supporters cannot trust what is real until this is reopened, is the kind of signal the manuscript has in mind, though it is one former captain's view and the page offers nothing on what supporters themselves think. It is also not clear that a fresh inquiry would settle anything; accounts given eight years on may be no more reliable than those given at the time.

What would turn this from a disagreement into a finding is reporting on what Cricket Australia actually holds, whether the people who ran the 2018 process were asked about the new claims, and any response from Mr Cummins, Mr Hazlewood or Mr Lyon, whom the paper does not quote beyond the 2021 statement.
