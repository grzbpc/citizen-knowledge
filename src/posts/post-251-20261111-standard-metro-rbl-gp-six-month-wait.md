### The story

Metro carries a two-page advertisement feature on pages 20 and 21, headed "A Legacy of Courage", for the Royal British Legion on Armistice Day. It tells the story of Liam Young, aged 40, a fourth-generation member of the Armed Forces who served in Afghanistan and Iraq. In 2007, while serving in Afghanistan, he suffered a perforated bowel and began to develop sepsis, and was flown back to a hospital in the Midlands, a long way from his home in County Durham. He says the Legion looked after his family then, making sure his father had somewhere to stay while he was in hospital and enough money to get by, and carrying out house checks on him when he came home. He was medically discharged 12 years ago and now works as a dangerous goods safety adviser with the US Air Force at Lakenheath in Suffolk.

Two years ago, he says, he needed the Legion again, this time for his mental health. He went to his GP first, who told him there was a six-month waiting period. He had what he calls a mini breakdown in her office, they talked, and she told him that as he was ex-service he should ring the charity. He rang the Legion's helpline and says that, as soon as he did, the whole system kicked in, and he was face to face with a therapist within two weeks. A box on the second page, headed "The Power of Your Poppy", says that Britain's largest Armed Forces charity relies on donations from the public to fund its work.

### The reframe

This is paid-for material, written to raise donations, and it should be read as such. Nothing in it is a criticism of the Legion, which by Mr Young's account acted quickly and well, or of the GP, who by his account found him a route to help. The entry does not question his account, which is one person's recollection of a conversation two years ago. A good outcome for him is a good outcome, and the entry takes it as one.

What the manuscript's chapter on charity draws attention to is a single sequence inside the story. A person in distress was quoted a long wait by one service, was told to go to a charity instead, and was seen quickly there. The manuscript describes charities as having become a preferred way of addressing harm, operating alongside public provision and sometimes in place of it, so that a problem is seen to be dealt with while the arrangements that produced it stay as they were. The page cannot show that this is what happened. A six-month wait is a figure recalled from one surgery. The page does not say how long the wait for the same service was across the area at the time, whether a quicker route was open to him, or whether the GP's suggestion was routine practice for former service personnel. It is also possible that the two routes are meant to work together, with the charity providing something the health service does not.

What the page does make plain is who carries the cost of the second door. The closing box says the charity cannot do this work without gifts from the public, and the feature is itself a request for them. The entry does not say that this is wrong. It notes only that, where a charity is the quicker route, the speed depends on donations, and that an advertisement feature is not the place where the question of who ought to provide this care would be asked.

What would test the point is data the page does not give: waiting times for talking therapies in his area in the period concerned, and how many former service personnel are referred to the Legion by GPs each year.
